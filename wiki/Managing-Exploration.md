## Managing Exploration

## Scale, Distance, and Time

To speed up exploration, you should split the world into different time and distance scales at different phases of the adventure. While traveling over large distances, hours and days are the default measure of time, and threemile leagues are the default measure of distance. A party traveling on foot can reasonably expect to cross one league of easy terrain per hour, and can maintain this pace without difficulty for 8 hours (or about 24 miles) per day. Most kingdom-scale maps should therefore be split into eight-league hexes, with each eight leagues of road or easy terrain representing a day’s travel.

### Leagues and Paces

Each eight-league hex should be further divided into one-league sub-hexes, to make it easier to keep track of the party’s overland progress from one hour to the next. Crossing eight leagues of road in a day does not require any real rolling or decisions, but traversing difficult terrain should be managed on an hour-by-hour basis.

Leagues with complex terrain or important features should be even further subdivided into half-mile exploration hexes, each of which (if not filled with difficult terrain) can be crossed at a walking pace during a tenminute exploration turn. Crossing an exploration hex full of difficult terrain will require two turns to cross - one full turn to enter into the hex, and another to exit.

Each exploration hex that contains a dungeon or lookout should refer to a dungeon map, drawn on a square grid that is split into fivefoot paces. The 5-foot pace is the smallest unit of distance typically used in the game, with player characters and monsters positioning themselves on the dungeon map grid during an encounter. Encounters measure time in sixsecond spans, allowing most player characters to traverse five paces during this span without special effort. Diagonal movement across a square grid requires special consideration; every two full paces of diagonal movement counts as three paces of straight movement.

### Other Distances

Strategists and tacticians often make maps with three other distances: the 20-pace *field*, the 100-pace *shot*, and the 500-pace *halfmile*.

### Making Maps

Standardizing your map-making process can be valuable. It's usually a good idea to create two different maps at each scale. Keep one for your own use, with all of the discoverable secrets and encounter probabilities noted, and provide a second mostly-unlabeled version to provide to the players. Published adventures for Materia Mundi will come in four different map scales:

#### Domain Map

A domain or wilderness map is divided into 24-mile *march* hexes, each of which is further subdivided into eight 3-mile *league* hexes. Remember that the maximum visibility on even terrain is three miles, so the party can, at best, see *into* adjacent league hexes from their current position, but cannot see *through* them.

#### Exploration Map

An exploration map "zooms in" on a single march hex, dividing it into half-mile exploration hexes. Each exploration hex represents the amount of area that the party can cross, or explore, in a single 10-minute exploration turn.

#### Battle Map

A battle map typically represents one ½-mile exploration hex, subdividing it into 100-foot (20-pace) "field" hexes. Large-scale battles and sieges will happen on a battle map, to give troop formations, siege weapons, and huge creatures space to maneuver and fire.

#### Encounter Map

An encounter or dungeon map typically represents a city, village, dungeon, or stretch of wilderness where precise positioning is likely to be important. The smallest encounter maps each represent a single 20-pace "field". Encounter maps are divided into squares, rather than hexes; each square represents one pace (5 feet). Some larger encounter maps will have 20-pace squares or hexes overlaid onto them, so that they can serve for both largescale battles and character-scale encounters.

### Passage of Time

Scaling time and distance proportionately makes for much easier accounting. The time scale of a particular phase of the adventure is the rate at which the party is invited to make decisions about what to do next and roll dice to determine the result of those decisions.

#### Days

For example, when operating on the domain or wilderness map with eight-league hexes, you should give the party a minute or two to decide which direction to travel in, and what morning and evening preparations to make.

Then the party’s navigator can make a Survival check (or another relevant task check) to determine whether the party gets lost or bogged down, has a random encounter, or discovers a feature within the hex that they are entering or leaving.

#### Hours

If the terrain or special features of a hex indicate the need for more detail, you should begin tracking the party’s movement through each league of travel, pausing at the top of each hour to ask the party what preparations they are making, and whether they wish to explore this league-hex more thoroughly or traverse into an adjacent hex. Once each member of the party has chosen their activity for the hour, you can have each of them make a single task check relevant to that activity. Highly detailed interactions or movement indicate that it’s time to switch scales to the exploration map with its half-mile (500 pace) hexes and 100-pace sub-hexes.

#### Turns

Once the party begins exploring the exploration map in depth, you should begin tracking individual ten-minute exploration turns – with each member of the party (and their henchmen) choosing a single exploration action to perform every ten minutes, and then rolling the appropriate task check to determine the outcome of that action.

#### Rounds

Then, if a combat encounter occurs, or some other event with a "ticking clock" of danger, switch to the combat rules with six-second encounter rounds.

## Torches, Lamps, and Light

Outdoors during the day, there is typically enough light for everyone to see clearly. In a dungeon, the party needs to bring their own light to be able to see what they are doing. A lantern typically casts bright light out to ten paces, and dim light out to another ten paces (twenty paces total), while a torch typically casts bright light out to five paces, and dim light out to another five paces (ten total). A full lamp will burn for roughly an hour, while a torch will burn for 1d6+1 exploration turns. Thus, torches and lanterns provide a natural set of distance and time measurements for the party when adventuring in the dungeon: a "lamp" is 20 paces (one field) of length, or one hour of time. Most civilized settlements, and some dungeons, will have braziers or hanging lamps placed at regular intervals along walls to illuminate well-traveled passageways.

Anything bright enough to illuminate is also bright enough to be seen in the darkness, from quite a ways away. Lighting a lamp is thus an excellent way to announce one's presence, and location, to everything within line of sight that might take an interest in the party's activities. Lanterns are typically sheltered from wind, rain, and minor jostling, while torches are much more exposed. Thus, in inclement weather or when moving quickly in the dungeon, a torch-bearer might need to make periodic Finesse checks to keep their torch from going out.

If the party runs out of torches, new ones can be fashioned from sticks, rags, and lamp oil without the need for a roll. One hour's worth of lamp oil soaked into a rag and wrapped around a forearm-sized stick is sufficient to create a single torch; while this is somewhat less efficient than burning the oil in a proper lamp, sometimes adventurers must make do.

## Travel Actions

Exploring the map can take as much or as little time as you and your party want. Some styles of play prefer to spend a good deal of time exploring the uncharted wilderness, while others consider the overland hex-map to simply be a distraction between the party and the dungeon.

### Navigation and Maps

Knowing where you are, and where you are going, requires a combination of different talents. Familiarity with terrain and local flora and fauna is governed by the Survival talent, while the ability to read maps and recognize landmarks is governed by Lore. A good navigator will have sufficient proficiency in both talents to keep the party heading in the right direction.

Recognizable landmarks and well-marked maps determine the difficulty for Lore checks to know where the party is, what direction is north when the stars are obscured, and what kinds of dangers are likely to be ahead on the path chosen. Survival checks are more grounded in the party’s immediate surroundings, and can give hints about dangers, challenges, or opportunities that might be within the current 1-league hex or its six adjacent hexes.

Characters in unfamiliar territory might wish to create their own maps, both for their own use and to gift or sell to other adventurers. A surveyor or map-maker will usually sketch a crude overview of the local terrain during travel, then revise and enhance it during rest. The general process can be distilled down to three task checks: Survival to notice all the relevant details, Lore to organize them into a readable map format, and Finesse to actually draw the map accurately.

### Traversing Difficult Terrain

While Lore and Survival are necessary for knowing the terrain, the ability to traverse it unharmed is a matter for saving throws.

Getting bogged down in swamps, treacherous mountain passes, or densely overgrown forests is a common fact of life for wandering adventurers, once the party leaves the wellmaintained roads behind and delves into the true unknown.

### Managing Supplies

The Finesse talent is also useful while traveling. Finesse is needed to maintain the party’s gear, to keep any wagons or pack animal loads balanced and secure across difficult terrain, and to come up with alternate solutions to Agility checks. The party should always be encouraged to come up with clever ways to traverse terrain – a clever solution might lower the difficulty of an Agility or Survival check, or might overcome the need for a roll altogether.

In certain circumstances, a Survival check might be necessary to coax animals through a path they would not normally wish to traverse or to steady their nerves in dangerous situations. The difficulty will depend on both the situation, and the number of animals being managed at once; a large pack might need multiple handlers.

### Stealth

A party moving at half speed can make Stealth tests to attempt to cover their tracks, move more quietly, and find less noticeable paths to traverse. Each member of the party must make the Stealth test; the lowest result determines the party’s success.

### Watching for Encounters

Anyone on watch uses their Alertness to help avoid or prepare for encounters, while the rest of the party focuses on navigation, load maintenance, and terrain management.

### Points of Interest

Each league of terrain might have a lookout or watchtower, or even a hidden dungeon worth exploring. Stopping and exploring a sub-hex can take anywhere from an hour to a whole day, depending on the terrain; Survival, Lore, Science and Alertness are all useful talents to apply to the task.

### Lookouts and Watchtowers

Finding a lookout or a watchtower allows the party to make Agility checks (or sometimes other task checks) to climb it and get a better view of the surroundings, or possibly take a much-needed rest. While at a lookout, the party’s line of sight is increased in various directions, allowing them to make Lore, Survival, and Alertness tests for hexes that they are not yet in or adjacent to.

## Vehicles and Mounts

A good riding mount allows a party to carry more gear without encumbrance and ignore some kinds of rough terrain, but does not otherwise increase travel speed. A fast horse might be able to double its overland speed for up to an hour – traveling up to 2 leagues at a full gallop - but would then need a full hour of rest. Pushing a horse past this limit is likely to wound or even kill it.

Hitching a team of horses to a wagon allows that wagon to travel at walking pace (one league per hour / 24 miles per day), while carrying several tons’ worth of gear and provisions. Wagons can only traverse roads or smooth ground, and quickly become bogged down in rough terrain. Crossing a river with a wagon typically involves fashioning some kind of raft and floating the wagon across – a task fraught with the potential for mishap.

Particularly inventive Wizards and Dwarves are likely to construct all manner of wondrous self-propelled vehicles, often capable of much faster land speeds.

### Seafaring and Flying

Most sizable sailing vessels can reach a cruising speed of approximately two leagues per hour in open water. As on land, inventive Enchanters might construct arcane propulsion systems capable of much higher speeds.

Unlike land travel, most ships can sail day and night, giving them 24 hours of travel per day at their rated speed.

Flying mounts (and flying vehicles) can make overland travel almost trivially easy. A flying party ignores terrain difficulty when determining their travel speed, although wind and inclement weather can create conditions roughly analogous to “difficult terrain”. The height advantage also gives the party a literal “eagle’s eye view”, letting them see much farther than when traveling along the ground - although weather might also interfere with this, and smaller terrain features are likely to be overlooked.

During an encounter, most vehicles and flying characters will have a turn radius larger than a one-pace square, with faster movement typically requiring an even wider turn to change directions.

### Managing Maps

Wilderness and dungeon maps are a crucial tool for maintaining the consistency of your game world. Maps serve as your “memory”

for the world, holding the locations of important landmarks, creatures, and events so that you don’t have to keep an entire world in your head at every moment.

### Arts & Crafts

Creating and using detailed wilderness, exploration, and encounter maps is an important skill for a referee to learn.

Kingdom, wilderness, and dungeon exploration maps can be made physically or digitally, depending on your preference as a referee, but “encounter maps” should usually be physical unless you are using a “virtual tabletop” to run the whole game. A physical encounter map, with physical miniatures (or even chits) to represent each monster and player character, helps the party quickly visualize the flow of an encounter. Even during exploration, these encounter maps are useful for helping the players understand the space that they are exploring and interacting with.

Maintaining maps at different scales can seem arduous at first, but the reward-to-effort ratio is surprisingly high. If you make a keyed “master dungeon map” at a scale of ¼” per pace, with each room individually labeled, you can then make a separate ½”-per-pace “encounter map” for each labeled room or hallway, with the edges of the encounter maps cut to the doorways between rooms.

Then, you can lay down individual rooms as the party enters them, and pick up rooms as they leave the party’s line of sight - providing a visual representation of the space for the party to orient to, without providing them with easy access to information that wouldn’t be immediately available to their characters. On your master dungeon map, you can use pushpins to denote where the party is, as well as any wandering monsters or other mobile threats. During each exploration turn, you can decide where each monster group moves based on its goals and personality, only checking for encounters whenever a monster group is close enough to the party to risk detection.

## Exploration Actions

It’s important to remember that many goals might be accomplished through several different approaches, each of which might call for a different task or saving throw.

For example, picking a lock or disabling a trap through fine manipulation of its mechanisms should be accomplished through a Finesse check; noticing that a merchant is lying about their participation in a historical event might involve a Lore test to recall the actual facts of the matter, an Intuition test to notice the gut feeling of dishonesty, or a Wit test to “spot a fellow liar”. In all cases, you should have the player describe to you - in detail - what their character tries, then set an appropriate difficulty and then either let them roll an appropriate task check, or declare that their solution is sufficient to succeed without a roll.

### Hidden Results

Sometimes the players will attempt actions where the results will not become apparent until some later time. In such cases, it is often useful to make the roll yourself, behind a “dungeon-master screen”, and only give the players a vague sense of how well their characters performed at their task until the time of revelation is at hand.

### Player vs. Character Knowledge

The philosophy of old-school dungeon crawl roleplaying games tends to favor the players' ingenuity over mechanical dice-rolling based on their characters' abilities and talents when possible. Sometimes this results in the players knowing things that their characters have no real way of knowing, and acting on this "outof-character" knowledge. In other situations, it might mean the players missing something that their characters would reasonably know or notice.

In general, if the player knows something that their character has any plausible means of also knowing, let them act on that knowledge. If a player knows something that their character is unlikely to know, *or* if a player is unaware of something that their character would most likely be familiar with, have the player make a relevant task check to see if their character actually knows the information, then tell them what they should know if their roll succeeds.

## Adjusting Difficulty

As a referee, one skill that you will want to develop quickly is the ability to roughly gauge the plausibility of any attempted action. The difficulty system has been divided into ‘easy, ‘normal’, and hard’ thresholds to make this easier, but you must ultimately use your own common sense.

### Skill, Tools, Time, and Approach

A good rule of thumb for any task is to consider the skill, tools, and time given for any given task, as a set of four yes/no questions:

- Does the character have the necessary

minimum skill to perform the task at hand?

- Do they have the right tools for the job?

- Does the character have sufficient time to

perform the task required?

- Is the character taking a reasonably sound

approach to the problem, based on the player’s description of their action?

#### Automatic

If the answer to all four of these questions is ‘yes’ - the character is sufficiently skilled, sufficiently equipped, has sufficient time, and is trying a reasonable action - then you generally shouldn’t make them roll at all.

Simply grant them success and move on, unless you have a particular need to determine “how well” they succeed (in which case the actual task you’re measuring is the attempt at an exceptional success).

#### Impossible

If they only have one ‘yes’ answer, then they need to find some advantage to even the odds, to be allowed to make the roll at all. Since they are already at impossible difficulty, a single sufficiently large advantage will cancel out one of them, letting them try a hard roll. Applying multiple advantages might allow them to make a roll at normal difficulty, but without either tools, time, or skill, they shouldn't be able to make the roll easy.

#### Task Check

If their approach is reasonable, and they have at least two other ‘yes’ answers - sufficient skill and tools while rushed, or sufficient skill and time but inadequate tools, or sufficient tools and time but insufficient skill - then a task check is called for. Use your intuition to determine the difficulty, based on the likelihood of success of a moderately skilled (4+) character with proper equipment within a 10-minute exploration turn, then have them roll their talent or ability.

#### Good Idea, Bad Idea

If the character’s approach is wrong, let them try whatever they’re trying and make a roll, then describe the actual consequences that would logically result from those actions.

Once they have dealt with those consequences, let them try to figure out why their approach isn’t working and try again if they still can. It is important to give them as much information as they could reasonably glean from each attempt; if you bog your players down in too many obscure situations, they will begin to feel like they need to “guess the referee’s password” to proceed, which no one ever finds fun.

### Rewarding Ingenuity

Remember that the referee’s job is to be fair, neither favoring the party nor the dungeon. If the party comes up with a clever idea that would conceivably work, it is important to let them try it, setting a difficulty based on your estimated likelihood of success and the suitability of their plan to the task at hand. Likewise, if the party comes up with a solution that is clever, effective, and certain or almost certain to work, don’t force them to roll simply because there is a relevant talent or ability that might apply. Just let them succeed and move on to the next turn of exploration.

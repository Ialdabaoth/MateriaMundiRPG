## Encounters

### Wandering Monsters

In a dungeon, tight corridors and enclosed spaces mean that there are far fewer places to be than in an open wilderness environment.

Monsters and monster groups wandering through the dungeon should have routine patrol paths, which are sequences of rooms that a given monster might show up in, along with the percentage of their time that they spend in each room.

A given monster group’s location can then be determined with a single table, to determine which room they are in – if a monster group winds up in the same room as the party, an encounter occurs, while if the group winds up in an adjacent room, an approaching encounter occurs. Most patrol paths can be broken into 5% increments, lending themselves easily to a single d20 table.

In addition to their patrol path, note each room that the monsters are likely to notice loud noises or bright lights, based on how close the patrol path passes by those rooms. These awareness zones will give you a ‘chance of noticing’ Alertness difficulty whenever the party gets careless with its stealth, to see which (if any) monsters notice the party’s shenanigans and show up to investigate.

Having monster groups with patrol paths and zones of awareness also helps you manage more general monster behavior. For example, if a monster breaks off and flees an encounter with the party, you can make a zone of awareness roll to see whether the fleeing monster finds another group of monsters to report to, and which group. This brings us to the third aspect: monsters in a dungeon should have default attitudes not just to the party, but to each other. This will help determine which groups will tend to reinforce each other, which are likely to snack on, bully, or otherwise predate each other, and which will mostly leave each other alone.

Once each of your monster groups has its patrol paths, zones of awareness, and attitudes and alliances worked out, you can reverse-engineer their tables into dungeon room tables with the equivalent statistical outcomes if you choose – or you can simply track the position of nearby groups in realtime on your own dungeon map, rolling each exploration turn to determine whether each nearby wandering monster group moves into a new room, and if so which one.

Here is an example patrol path for a monster group made up of 2d6 goblins, in a dungeon with a ring of rooms that link A-B-C-D-E-A, and an alternate path from B to D:

Goblin Patrol starting location (d20)

| | |
|---|---|
| 1-4 | Room A (move to: E 8-13, B 14-20) |
| 5-8 | Room B (move to: A 9-12, B 13-16, D 17-20) |
| 9-12 | Room C (move to: B 8-13, D 14-20) |
| 13-16 | Room D (move to: B 9-12, C 13-16, E 17-20) |
| 17-20 | Room E (move to: D 8-13, A 14-20) |

Goblin Patrol Alertness difficulty

| Room A: Hard | Room C: Hard | Room E: Hard |
|---|---|---|
| Room B: Normal | Room D: Normal |  |

This will result in a patrol that stays in its current room about 33% of the time, and otherwise has an equal chance of moving into any adjacent room. ### Monster Lairs

Various monsters and monster groups should have lairs, which are areas that they are familiar with and connected to. A wandering monster will spend a good deal of its time in its lair, and will usually retreat to its lair when injured.

A monster in its lair should find most of its attacks and defenses easier, and will typically have various special maneuvers or feats that it can only perform within its lair. Some of these will be at-will, but especially powerful lair feats should use a lair pool equal to the monster’s hit dice. Monsters that already have an encounter pool for their personal feats can use their lair pool for both their lair powers and their personal encounter powers while in their lair.

If you’re having trouble designing a monster lair, you can use the following d20 table for inspiration:

Monster Lair Features (D6)

| | |
|---|---|
| 1-2 | Natural Hazard - whether through fey magic or some other means, the monster controls its environment sufficiently to exploit natural elemental hazards, such as lava, floods, earthquakes, grasping vines, or the like. The monster can use its lair pool to activate or control these hazards at various opportune moments. |
| 3 | Hiding Spots - the monster has one or more tunnels, hidden passageways, or ambush locations that it can use to quickly become hidden, then reappear at another hiding spot to ambush the party. |
| 4 | Clever Traps - the monster or its minions have set up various mechanical or magical traps through its lair, which can be activated or controlled with the monster’s lair pool. |
| 5 | Rejuvenation - the monster has a feature that allows it to heal hp and regenerate its lair pool while it occupies its lair. |
| 6 | Roll twice and add both features. |

### Boss Fights

A “boss fight” is a centerpiece monster of a particular dungeon; these creatures usually have much higher hit dice than the dungeon’s average wandering monster, and often will have hit dice that rival or surpass even the toughest monster groups taken together.

It can be tempting to build each dungeon around a boss fight, but it can also be rewarding to have dungeons where the primary danger comes from a trap or hazard, or an unending swarm of identical creatures. If you do decide to focus your dungeon around one or more ‘boss monsters’, be sure that they fit the theme and tone of the dungeon, and consider what kind of lairs they might set up.

A boss will often define the entire ‘theme’ of a dungeon; randomly deciding on a boss is often a good starting point to build a dungeon encounter around, should you find yourself bereft of ideas.

Dungeon Boss (2D6)

| | |
|---|---|
| 2 | Eye of Yog-Sothoth with a random assortment of magically mutated minions |
| 3 | Primordial Dragon with lizardfolk and serpent-centaur minions. |
| 4-5 | Fair Folk with various fey beast, natural animal, and enthralled mortal minions |
| 6-7 | Ogre with various goblin minions. |

| 10 | Vampire with various undead and enthralled mortal minions |
| 11 | Baal-Malak with a mix of infernal and mortal cultist minions |

### Monster Groups

The party will rarely meet a single monster or NPC alone. Encounter group tables are a way for the referee to organize groups of monsters into troupes, mobs, or swarms, which are likely to appear together during an adventure with a frequency that depends on  the environment.

When you design an adventure, you can use the following encounter group tables as presented, or use them as inspiration to build your own.

#### Wild Animals

By far the most common creatures to encounter are wild animals. Different kinds of terrain will have different frequencies of animals; by default, roll 2d6 to determine what kind of animals the group encounters:

2-6 Wolf Pack, 7 Dire Wolf Pack, 8-9 Bear, 1012 Beast Herd.

**Wolf Pack** A wolf pack will contain 2d6 wolves, which will engage in pack tactics to harass the party if they are hunting and the party seems weak or poorly defended.

**Dire Wolf Pack** A dire wolf pack will contain 1d6 dire wolves and 1d6 dire wolf pups (treat as wolves), which will engage in pack tactics to ambush the party unless the party’s total challenge rating exceeds their own.

**Bear** Roll 1d6 to determine species: 1-3 black bear, 4-6 grizzly bear. On a ‘1’ or ‘6’ the bear is accompanied by 1d6 cubs (treat as small bears of the same species with 2 hit dice). A lone bear will likely be wary of travellers, while a bear with cubs will be ferocious and unpredictable.

**Beast Herd** A beast herd will consist of 2d6 creatures of the same species, determined by rolling a d6: 1-2 elk, 3-4 ox, 5-6 horse.

#### The Undead

Areas where atrocities have occurred sometimes become haunted with the yin emanations of the Shadow. Unless cleansed and hallowed by a priest, creatures that die in these unholy lands will eventually become animated by these eldritch energies, becoming undead abominations. You can roll 2d6 to determine the composition of an undead horde (2-6 Skeleton Pack, 7-9 Zombie Horde, 10-11 Feral Revenants, 12 Vampire), **Skeleton Pack** A skeleton platoon will typically contain 1d6 2 hd Medium skeletons with pikes, shields, and chainmail, + 1d6 1 hd skeletons in leather with longbows and shortswords.

**Shambler Horde** A shambler horde most often contains 2d6 1 hd Medium humanoid shamblers. Roll an additional d6; on a ‘6’, the pack contains a 3hd Large creature, such as a bear or a dire wolf. **Feral Revenants** A single 1d6+2 hd feral revenant will usually be accompanied by a skeleton pack, plus an additional 3 hd Large zombie or skeleton mount.

**Vampire** Vampires are best constructed as unique NPCs, each with a backstory and a place in a larger web of intrigues.

To make a random vampire NPC, roll up its abilities as if creating a new character, but roll 4d6 and drop the lowest die for each ability, then arrange them in any order that best suits their role as an antagonist.

**Wild Hunt** The party might stumble onto the Wild Hunt, an eldritch Fair Folk ritual incursion into the Material. The Wild Hunt will consist of a Keryne being hunted by a hunting party of 1d6+1 Dire Wolves led by a Horned Hunter riding a Nightmare. The party might stumble onto any one of these creatures alone or together, at which point the entire Wild Hunt will reconfigure itself with the party as the prey, and the Keryne attempting to lure or trick them into the Hunter and Dire Wolves’ clutches.

The Horned Hunter’s legendary knife and greatbow will resize to fit any medium or small creature that survives being its prey by defeating it in single combat. Each weapon may be wielded by such a creature without penalty, but will still deal doubled damage as if they were wielded by a large creature.

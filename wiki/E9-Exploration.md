A party of adventurers explores a dungeon in ten-minute intervals, called **exploration turns**. During an exploration turn, each player describes to the referee what action their character is performing, then the referee dialogs with them until both sides agree about what is being attempted. The referee then has each character roll whichever of their character's [talents](E9-Talents) the referee deems most appropriate for that action, against a difficulty determined by the nature of the task being attempted. This continues until each player character has performed their action, and then the referee describes the results of the party's actions and a new exploration turn begins.

Exploration is not limited to the dungeon; the party is welcome to explore areas of open wilderness as well. When they do, the "dungeon" referred to on this page means an "exploration zone" about a hundred paces across in any direction (see [Exploring a Hex](E9-Overland-Travel#exploring-a-hex)).

## Exploration Actions

Players should be encouraged to describe their characters' actions as creatively and thoroughly as possible. The majority of an exploration turn should happen in a freeform fashion, with the referee describing the basics of what the party immediately sees, hears, smells, and so on as the result of their last actions, and the party then deciding what to do about that information.

Whenever possible, the referee's descriptions will be everything that the player characters can directly perceive, mediated by their Alertness and the limitations of their senses; the players' descriptions will be what their characters do for the next 10 minutes, described in free-form. The referee then determines for each character's action whether a task check is called for, and directs their players to make any required rolls. Then the next round of exploration begins with the referee's next set of descriptions, which the players respond to with their next set of actions, and so on.

## Torchlight and Visibility

A typical lantern provides enough light to illuminate 10 paces in every direction reasonably well, and illuminate out to 20 paces dimly. This sets a natural movement speed for dungeon exploration at about 100 feet (20 paces) per exploration turn, limited more by safety and visibility than actual speed. In daylight in the wilderness, the party can reasonably move through up to a half mile (500 paces) per exploration turn.

Torches typically last 1d6+1 exploration turns, but might require a [Finesse](E9-Talents#finesse) check to stay lit if the party is moving quickly or performing complex and risky activities like climbing cliff faces or dodging traps. Lanterns usually last a full day on one flask of oil, and are easier to keep lit during activities.

## Entering a New Area

When the party first enters a new area of the dungeon, the referee should describe to them what they experience at first glance. No rolls are made at this time, but the referee adjusts the level of detail of their description based on the lighting and the characters' Alertness; better Alertness means more small details are noticed immediately. Anything "important-looking" within 50 feet (10 paces) or so should be described in moderate detail, with brief descriptions of anything further away, depending on the lighting conditions.

## Moving Around

Within an area, the characters can move relatively freely during each exploration turn. A character can move 20 paces and still perform an action, or move 100 times their dash speed as their full exploration action. Areas of difficult terrain, or areas that require extensive climbing, swimming, or jumping to get to, might require an [Agility](E9-Talents#agility) check to navigate.

If the player characters' movements activate a trap, a hazard, or a random encounter with monsters, the referee stops the action mid-round and describes what is happening, then switches to a [combat encounter](E9-Combat).

## Stealth

The party may choose to move at half speed in order to employ stealth. Each member of the party rolls [Stealth](E9-Talents#stealth), and each monster that is on watch that might notice them rolls [Alertness](E9-Talents#alertness). Medium or heavy armor and being encumbered each make Stealth harder, while intervening walls and distracting background noises make Alertness harder. Experts can engage in stealth without interfering with other actions, while fighters and magic-users find both their Stealth check and any other check they make harder if they try to perform another action while sneaking.

The party manages to avoid alerting nearby monsters with the sound of their movements either if everyone's Stealth check succeeds, or if each monster's Alertness check fails. If at least one Stealth roll fails and at least one Alertness check succeeds, though, the monsters are alerted and will move to investigate.

Torchlight and other sources of illumination are another matter. Wandering monsters that can see will obviously be alerted to the party's presence if they can see the party's lights and shadows flickering along the walls; choosing between seeing and being seen is a problem that the party will have to figure out as they explore the dungeon.

## Watching for Trouble

It's a good idea to have at least one member of the party "on watch" during each round of exploration. Each character on watch rolls an Alertness check to spot any approaching trouble. Posting a watch means that if a random encounter occurs, at least someone in the party is likely to be able to act without being surprised. Monster groups will also typically have a few creatures designated as "on watch", which the referee will manage.

## Random Encounters

Encounters can happen randomly, either while navigating the wilderness or exploring the dungeon. The referee should make a random encounter roll after the party declares their actions. The referee secretly rolls a single d6, or possibly two d6s and chooses the higher or lower die based on the amount of noise, smell, light, and so on that the party is making. If no event is rolled, then the party continues on with their next round of exploration.

| d6 | Encounter check |
|---|---|
| 1-4 | No event |
| 5 | Approaching encounter (within 10 minutes) |
| 6 | Random encounter! |

### Starting an Encounter

If an encounter or approaching encounter is rolled, the referee rolls on the appropriate random encounter table for the party's current location to determine the encounter group, then determines which members of the encounter group were on watch, and whether that group was moving stealthily. The referee then makes Alertness and Stealth checks for the encounter group and compares them with the Alertness and Stealth checks of the party during the exploration turn, to see whether either group notices the other.

If an **approaching encounter** is rolled, any party that has been alerted to the other has a full exploration turn to prepare for the upcoming encounter, and the Alertness checks of anyone "on watch" at this time are easier. If neither party was alerted to the other (due to everyone succeeding at Stealth checks and failing at Alertness checks), then both parties stumble into each other as if the random encounter result had been rolled instead.

If the **random encounter** result is rolled, the referee announces the encounter to the party, adjusting the level of detail based on the net results of the Stealth and Alertness checks made immediately prior. Creatures whose Stealth or Alertness checks succeed get to act in an additional first round of combat, called the **surprise round**, while everyone else is effectively stunned. After that round, everyone on both sides declares their actions and acts in each subsequent round.

## Searching for Traps, Hazards, and Secrets

One of the more common actions during exploration is searching for, and disabling or otherwise overcoming, traps and hazards within the dungeon. A **hazard** is a natural difficulty within the dungeon that must be overcome, such as a crumbling stone bridge or a deep chasm with sheer walls, while a **trap** is a deliberately constructed hazard, usually with some mechanism of activation.

Traps and hazards may be obvious or hidden. An obvious trap or hazard is noticeable as soon as the party has line of sight to it; any hidden traps or hazards must be detected with Alertness checks.

Generally, the process of searching for hidden features should be spelled out in detail (if a player declares "I search for traps" as their action, the proper response from the referee should be "okay, how?"), with those details interacting with the nature of the trap to determine difficulty. A well-hidden trap might be naturally hard to search for, but might be easy instead if the player notices the right details from the room's description and focuses their attention in the right place.

Properly described searches might very well set a difficulty of automatic, with no roll necessary; if the players figure out a particular puzzle simply from the initial description given to them, the referee should simply reward them for their ingenuity and move on. Likewise, if they are simply not searching in the right area or using the right methods at all, the difficulty is impossible and no d6 task check is required. It's only when a search action has some degree of risk or chance involved that a player should roll their character's Alertness (or possibly another talent, such as Finesse or Science) to see if they succeed.

An Alertness check to search for traps also searches for hidden doors or passages; if a door is discovered, it may turn out to be open, locked, barred, or blocked. Finding a hidden door is much like finding a hidden trap; there is a default difficulty for noticing it during any search, while a search that focuses on the right area and uses the right methods should be easier or even automatic.

## Overcoming Natural Hazards

Most natural hazards can be avoided or overcome with an Agility check or a Strength check. Clever solutions might instead involve "dungeoneering" something clever out of available materials and resources; the referee assigns d6 task checks and difficulties appropriate to the players' descriptions.

## Kicking In Doors and Moving Obstacles

Barred doors, blocked hallways, and the like often have no way to "pick" them; instead, they must be overcome with Strength checks, in a fashion similar to overcoming a hazard. Unlike a hazard, failing to overcome a barred door or blocked hallway usually does not trigger an attack or inflict damage, unless there is a hidden hazard or trap built into the blockage.

The threshold for the Strength check depends on the obstruction: breaking down a thin wooden door might be a 3+, while clearing a massive boulder wedged into a cave passage could be as high as 7+. Applying leverage might make a Strength check easier or harder depending on the method used, based on the referee's judgment. Multiple creatures can apply their strength at once to such a task; each creature's attempt might be easy or hard based on their technique and positioning, and then their check results all add together vs. the obstacle's threshold.

## Disarming Traps

Traps with at least some physical parts can usually be disabled with a [Finesse](E9-Talents#finesse) check. The difficulty of this check is determined by the complexity of the hazard or trap, and by how much effort the trap-maker went to conceal its inner workings.

If the party stops to examine the trap in detail first, a successful [Science](E9-Talents#science) check might reveal some clues as to its principle of operation, which might suggest specific approaches to disarming it that make the Finesse check to do so easier.

## Picking Locks

A locked door, whether hidden or obvious, must either be unlocked with the proper key, picked, or bashed in. Unlocking a lock with the proper key is usually a simple task, but might occasionally require a Finesse check to get the sequence right. Picking a lock operates identically to disarming a trap: mechanical locks are first inspected, then picked with a Finesse roll.

Obvious locks will sometimes have hidden traps, which activate and attack anyone trying to inspect or pick them. If no one explicitly searches the lock for hidden traps, then they activate them automatically when they first attempt to pick the lock. Otherwise, disabling the trap before picking the lock proceeds normally, after which the lock may be safely picked.

> **Finesse Expertise**
> Picking locks and disabling traps is part of the [Expert](E9-Expert#pick-lock--disarm-mechanism)'s basic class training. As such, the difficulty for picking a lock or disabling a trap is assumed to be the difficulty for an expert to attempt it; anyone without this expertise will at least have a harder time with their Finesse check, assuming they can even make the attempt at all.

## Dungeoneering

The party may create obstructions and traps, rather than removing them. Barring doors or blocking passages with heavy objects are typically feats of strength, while locking doors is often as simple as turning a latch, although some locks may need to be "unpicked" back into a locking position if the key isn't found. Various magics can make the creation and clearing of hazards trivial, if the party chooses to expend their limited magical resources in that fashion.

Whenever the party adjusts the layout of the dungeon, either by opening new passageways or blocking them off, the referee should note the changes on their map and adjust the behavior of any patrolling or wandering monsters as necessary. Wise adventurers will be careful about leaving a trail through the dungeon, which hostile monsters might use to track them down and ambush them.

Hiding the results of dungeoneering is itself a dungeoneering task, creating a situation where the monsters now need to be as wary of hidden traps as the players. Of course, if the players don't bother to mark on their maps where they placed hidden traps, or don't bother to check those maps when re-entering a room, it is certainly not the referee's job to remind them.

### Crafting Traps and Hazards

Players can, of course, construct traps as well as disabling them. Constructing a mechanical trap begins with the player describing the kind of trap they wish to construct, followed by a Science check to design the mechanisms, followed by a series of Finesse checks to construct each component. Feats of strength might also be required to dig out areas or haul large components into place.

Once the trap is set up, the referee determines the base difficulties to detect, inspect, and disable the trap, and the nature of the attack that the trap will perform, based on the results of the designer's Science check and the builders' Finesse checks. If the designer stays to inspect the work as it progresses, the referee should give them feedback about the detect, inspect, and disable difficulties, and the details of the trap's attack, to allow them to make adjustments to the design or focus more attention on specific sections to improve them.

### Repairing Ruined Wonders

One of the primary tasks for an adventuring team will be appraising the condition of ruined wonders, and, if possible, restoring them to their former glory. Understanding the purpose of a ruined wonder is a [Lore](E9-Talents#lore) check; inspecting it for damage, once its purpose is understood, is a Science check. Succeeding at both gives the party some idea of what repairs will be necessary, and what level of functionality can be expected if they succeed.

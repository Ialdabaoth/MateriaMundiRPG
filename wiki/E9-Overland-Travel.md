The first step in any adventure story is getting there. The world in a fantasy adventure game is a vast and dangerous place which must be navigated carefully. Ancient roads built by long-forgotten empires may cross the landscape, but often no one has maintained them for generations, and any that are well-traveled enough to be clear of debris and animal predators often hide bandits and cutthroats instead.

## Travel Shifts

Overland travel happens in **four-hour shifts**. Each day of travel has a budget of about 8 hours of labor that can be reasonably expected of the party, split into two such shifts; any more than that and the party can expect to become fatigued in short order.

A typical day of travel looks like this: the party wakes up about an hour before dawn and begins the normal daily preparation, including donning armor, preparing spells or prayers, or any other relevant pre-adventuring activities. After two hours of preparation and breakfast, camp is broken down over another hour, and the party readies for travel. The party then travels steadily for four hours, then breaks for two hours to eat, rest, and consult their maps. Then comes a second shift of travel, and then the party spends an hour setting up camp before retiring for the evening.

## Marches and Leagues

Maps are typically divided into hexes for convenience, with each large hex measuring a **twenty-four-mile march**, subdivided into eight **three-mile leagues**. On a well-traveled road with no hazards or interruptions, a well-stocked adventuring party can expect to cross a single league in about an hour, and a single march in one day.

Difficult terrain, heavy burdens, or bad weather might cause the party to become bogged down within a hex, requiring an [Agility](E9-Talents#agility) or [Survival](E9-Talents#survival) check to make any further progress. Each member of the party must succeed for the group to be able to advance; the group may attempt clever solutions to make the roll easier or aid the rolls of the less athletically-inclined members.

## Navigating

Before each four-hour shift of travel, the party should declare who is navigating, who is keeping watch, and who is attending to other duties. The referee makes a secret Survival check on behalf of each navigator at the start of the shift, to determine whether the party becomes lost.

### Stealthy Travel

The party may choose to employ stealth while traveling, halving their speed. For each league of travel that the party moves through stealthily, each character rolls a [Stealth](E9-Talents#stealth) check with difficulty determined by the terrain and each character's armor and encumbrance. If everyone in the party succeeds at their Stealth checks, they surprise any random encounter that they stumble across. Experts can engage in stealth without interfering with other actions, while fighters and magic-users find both their Stealth check and any other check they make harder if they try to perform another action while sneaking.

### Forced March

The party can attempt to force-march through a league, allowing them to attempt two league-crossings in one hour; each time they do so, each member of the party must succeed at a Survival check or suffer 1d6 damage. Terrain and the encumbrance of each party member might make this check easier or harder; it gets consecutively harder for each additional shift of travel without a full day of rest, or each further hour of forced march.

### Third Shift

In addition to forced march, a party may attempt a third shift of travel, granting them an additional 4 hours of travel in a day. A third shift also requires a Survival check to avoid damage.

## Random Encounters While Traveling

Each league of travel comes with the chance of a random encounter. The nature of those potential encounters, and the probability that they occur, is different from one league to another. The referee will typically have a random encounter table that lists the chance of encounter for a given area, and will roll one or two d6s for each league of travel; if an encounter occurs, travel stops as the party deals with the situation.

If either party succeeded at moving stealthily, then each member of the other party who was keeping watch rolls [Alertness](E9-Talents#alertness). If they all fail, they are surprised by the encounter. See [Random Encounters](E9-Exploration#random-encounters).

## Line of Sight

Line of sight on perfectly flat terrain and on a clear day only extends for about a league. This means that, barring terrain or weather, the party can usually see midway into the next hex when standing in the middle of a hex, and can fully survey an adjacent hex when standing at its border.

Of course, perfectly flat terrain and perfectly fair weather are almost unheard of deep in the wilds. Fog and precipitation will obscure line of sight, preventing the party from seeing out of their hex; vegetation and various terrain features will also be noted as blocking line of sight across a hex boundary.

Surveying an adjacent hex will likely make the party's Survival check to cross that league easier. The referee is encouraged to be descriptive when detailing the landscape, and the party is encouraged to be descriptive in how they intend to overcome the challenges that they see.

## Lookouts

Various points on the map will be designated as lookouts. A lookout is an elevated vantage point that the party could reasonably get to and survey the local landscape.

Weather permitting, a lookout allows the party to see one or even two leagues away in certain directions. A lookout notes both what directions it provides a view in, and the difficulty of any Agility check needed for a creature to ascend it (easy, normal, or hard). Detouring onto a lookout typically costs about an hour of travel.

Artificial structures such as watchtowers also dot the landscape. They provide stairs or ladders to easily scale them, and serve as lookouts in all directions when the weather is clear. If a member of the party has a device such as a spyglass, then using it atop a watchtower might allow them to thoroughly survey an adjacent hex, automatically noticing any terrain features or points of interest that aren't expressly hidden without the need for a task check.

## Exploring a Hex

Sometimes, a particular one-league hex merits closer attention. The [[Exploration|E9-Exploration]] page describes the process primarily as it pertains to a dungeon setting, but any exploration action can also be taken in the wilds. Generally, the referee will subdivide a one-league hex map into half-mile **sub-hexes** and again into 100-pace **exploration zones**. The party can cross five exploration zones (one sub-hex) of clear terrain in one ten-minute exploration turn at a walking pace, or they can spend an entire ten minutes thoroughly investigating a single exploration zone (rolling Alertness, Survival, or any other necessary talents).

Many adventures expect the party to explore various hexes, in order to discover hidden lookouts or dungeon entrances.

## Tracking, Foraging, and Hunting

Within an exploration zone, characters can forage for food or other resources, or can search for tracks or other clues that another creature has passed through the area. Each of these activities is an exploration action that requires a Survival check, with a difficulty based on the circumstances and the terrain being searched. For example, finding tracks on otherwise untouched snow or similar soft ground is easy, while finding tracks on bare rock with no other clues is hard. Fresh tracks that were laid within the last hour are easier to find, while tracks laid a day or more ago are harder.

Once tracks are found, gathering information from them is another exploration action and Survival check, at a difficulty one step harder than finding the tracks. If the character who finds the tracks has expertise in Survival, that expertise allows them to identify and gather all relevant information from a set of tracks in the same exploration action that they find them. The information that can be gathered includes the kind of creature or object that made the track, how old the tracks are, what direction they went in, and any other information the referee deems discoverable.

Foraging and hunting within an exploration zone is also an exploration action requiring a Survival check, with a difficulty that depends on both the terrain and the abundance of the resource being gathered.

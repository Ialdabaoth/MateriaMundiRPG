## Crafting

In addition to purchasing items, adventurers can also spend their time crafting their own. Any player character familiar with the correct crafting tools can use those tools within a suitable workshop to craft an item out of available raw materials.

### Crafting Weapons

Crafting a decent metal weapon typically takes about a day. A skilled artisan can produce one high-quality weapon with a day’s labor and quality materials and a successful easy Finesse roll, or one adequate weapon per hour with an average Finesse roll if they have adequate materials and facilities. If a crafter spends an entire day’s labor on a single weapon with the intent to produce a masterwork, they succeed with a hard Finesse roll. A masterwork weapon adds a +1 bonus to either its to-hit throw or its damage roll. Producing ammunition for ranged weapons is much simpler; a crafter may produce 1d6 arrows or crossbow bolts after one hour of effort with an easy Finesse roll.

### Crafting Armor

Crafting decent armor typically takes about a week, and the armor must be tailored to its intended wearer. At the end of the process, the craftsman makes an easy Finesse test to determine whether the armor is adequate; on a double success, they manage to produce a masterwork armor with a +1 bonus to its damage reduction if the armor’s raw materials were of sufficiently high quality. Mastercraft armor is always fitted to a particular creature, and must be re-tailored for any other being to gain its masterwork bonus.

Repairing sundered armor, or modifying an existing piece of armor to fit a new wearer, is a simpler task which takes approximately a day. Repairs require materials of the same quality as those used in the original crafting of the armor, or the armor will lose any masterwork bonus that it has.

A craftsman can typically sell a weapon or armor at prevailing market rates, but must then pay local taxes (or equivalents) or risk trouble. Masterwork weapons and armors can usually be sold for many times the cost of a merely adequate item.

### Crafting Other Gear

The referee is the ultimate arbiter of what kinds of equipment can be crafted, how long the process should take, and what tools, facilities and materials are required. Various tools and supplies can be manufactured with sufficient time and effort; many will require a Finesse check to successfully assemble, while others succeed automatically as long as the proper time and attention are paid. Medical supplies, non-perishable meals, and the like might require a Medicine or Survival check instead of a Finesse check.

Equipment can be manufactured that makes particular tasks easier, such as rappelling gear to assist with climbing actions (making Agility checks related to climbing easier). The referee’s common sense should guide what kinds of gear to allow in their campaign, and at what costs.

### Selling Crafted Items

An adventurer talented in crafting useful items can make a decent living selling them to other adventurers, nobles, or other buyers of questionable provenance. The process is the same as finding a merchant or artisan, but in reverse – the player characters must find a buyer that wishes to acquire the goods, then haggle until a deal agreeable to both sides has been reached.

Feudal lords might expressly commission a particularly talented craftsman to make an extravagant custom work, often providing the materials and paying handsomely for the labor. If the desired item requires rare alchemical supplies, the noble might expect the player character’s party to go questing for them as part of the commission. Such a request might form the basis of an entire adventure or series of adventures.

Once an adventurer has a reputation for selling high-quality wares, other buyers will likely seek them out more regularly, eventually providing a steady side income to supplement an adventuring party’s primary activities.

## Research

Adventurers spend a good deal of their downtime activities researching ancient maps and tomes, either in search of arcane knowledge or the ideal location for their next adventure. A research project will involve a series of task checks, with difficulties determined by the available research material. Science, Lore, Medicine, and Survival are all appropriate talents to use during a research montage, each covering their own domains of knowledge.

Of course, gathering that material from musty libraries or private collectors will also require adventuring or bargaining; during the course of a research project, the party might uncover references to documents not yet in their possession, leading to a pause in the research while they attempt to acquire them.

Players and referees are encouraged to roleplay out as much of the research as they can, so long as it does not slow down the flow of play. Clever ideas or unusual interpretations of facts might open up new directions for the campaign, even if they turn out to be wildly off-base once tested in the field.

Above all else, the game should never devolve into a monotonous series of dice rolls! As with every other aspect of play, the referee should describe the situation in free-form, and then the players should describe their characters' ideas, hunches, and research directions – also in free-form. Only then should the Referee determine a talent and difficulty, with the result filtering the players' ideas into the correct facts (or into the more wildly incorrect parts of their ideas, in the case of failures).

### Magic and Engineering

One common avenue of research is the design of new magic item enchantments, arcane spells, or alchemical formulae. Basic rules for spell design and magic item creation are included in these rules, but players are encouraged to be as creative and innovative as possible, with the referee ultimately deciding what is or isn't possible within the confines of their own campaign.

## Recuperating From Injuries

Injured creatures that are not poisoned or diseased will regain hit points slowly, over several days (or even weeks) of rest. Roll one hit die, then consult the following chart based on the result and the character’s level to determine the total number of days of rest needed to heal the next hit point:

| | | | | | | | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hit Die Result: |  | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6+ |
| Level 1 | 14 | 7 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 |  |
| Level 2 | 7 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 1 |  |
| Level 3 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 |  |
| Level 4 | 4 | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | ½ |  |
| Level 5 | 3 | 2 | 1 | 1 | ½ | ½ |  |
| Level 6+ |  | 2 | 1 | 1 | ½ | ½ | ⅓ |

Any strenuous activity will completely undo the current recuperation, requiring a new hit die roll. Another character may make a Medicine roll once per day to attempt to help tend to their wounds; the difficulty of this roll is based on the available medical supplies and facilities. Success reduces their recuperation time by one day, or automatically heals an additional hit point if the recuperation time is already less than one day. Once the injured character has rested the necessary number of days, they heal one hit point and roll again.

### Recovering from Poison or Disease

A poisoned or diseased character must first shake off their condition before they can heal any hit points. Different poisons and diseases have different rates of progression; some progress over the course of hours, others days, weeks, or even months. These rules are for truly debilitating lingering infections or toxins; anything that can be more-or-less "lived with" might impart penalties, but will not prevent normal rest and healing.

A lingering disease or toxin will list its progression rate, saving throw difficulty, and the number of successful saves necessary to fully shake it off, as well as its symptoms. For the duration that a character is seriously sick or poisoned, they must make a saving throw every span of time based on the progression rate of the disease or toxin (days, weeks, or months). Failure means they suffer 1d6 damage and worsen; success means they make progress towards recovery.

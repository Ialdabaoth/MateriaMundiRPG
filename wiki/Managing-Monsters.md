## Managing Monsters

During each 10-minute exploration turn, the various monsters in a dungeon are also actively going about their day. Each monster performs its own exploration actions, including moving, wandering and resting.

Monsters that are wandering the dungeon have a chance of running into the player characters, starting a random encounter.

Injured monsters that are resting to restore hit points tend to have a specific room, called their lair, that they retreat into to do so. If the player characters stumble on an injured monster in its lair, they interrupt its rest, but a monster in its lair almost always finds  its attacks and defenses easier due to its homeground advantage, and a cornered monster typically fights with unexpected ferocity.

## Monster Abilities

Each monster’s hit dice and abilities determine its attack throw thresholds and saving throw thresholds identically to a player character’s level and abilities. Note that a monster’s abilities can have a much wider range than a player character’s; even so, no monster’s saving throw or attack throw should ever be worse than 17+ or better than 5+.

Monster abilities can vary from individual to individual, just like a player character’s. A “typical” monster will have the abilities listed, while the referee might choose to roll or assign adjustments to one or more abilities for any unique or recurring NPC monster.

Monster abilities tend to vary somewhat less than mortals', so usually only a single d6 should be rolled per ability — a ‘1’ indicates an ability one lower than typical, or a ‘6’ indicates an ability one higher than typical. Finally, a monster’s Intelligence should be reflected in its behavior. Any monster with an Intelligence below -3 is non-sapient, possessing only animal levels of intelligence; any monster with an Intelligence of -3 or higher can reason as well as a player character with the equivalent Intelligence.

## Monster Attitudes

All monsters and NPCs have a default attitude towards the party, which guides their behavior in lieu of more detailed information. A monster or NPC’s attitude can be one of five levels:

A **helpful** monster or NPC will aid the player characters without hesitation, and will do as the player characters ask unless doing so is obviously harmful. Even harmful orders or requests may be followed, depending on the details of the creature’s relationship to the player characters.

A **friendly** monster or NPC will be disposed to trusting and aiding the player characters if doing so isn’t out of its way, or if it has sufficient incentive. It won’t risk its own safety or well-being for the player characters’ sake.

An **indifferent** monster or NPC has no particular interest in the player characters one way or the other, and behaves as its wariness, curiosity or boredom guide it to.

An **unfriendly** monster or NPC is fearful, annoyed, or distrustful of the player characters, and might seek to harm them if it thinks it can get away with doing so without too much trouble.

A **hostile** monster or NPC is actively aggressive towards the player characters, and will likely attack them on sight.

Bearing, Charm and Wit can be used in an exploration turn to engage with monsters that aren’t actively hostile; success or failure on task tests with these talents might move a monster’s attitude up or down one level, while exceptional success or failure might move a monster’s attitude up two levels at once. Other talents might aid this process, depending on how they are used. For more rapid adjustments, various mind-altering magic spells can quickly influence a creature’s attitude, but often result in a similar adjustment in the opposite direction when they wear off.

## Monsters in Combat

In a game about heroes exploring ancient dungeons, the party is eventually going to wind up having to fight some monsters.

### Monster Hit Dice

Monsters measure their overall toughness and experience using hit dice, just like player characters. Powerful monsters can exceed the threat level of even a fifth-level character, so monsters can range from half a hit die up to ten hit dice.

To figure out the total ‘threat budget’ of a monster encounter, add up the players’ total class levels, including the class levels or hit dice of any retinue. The total monster hit dice in the encounter should approximately equal the total party hit dice, if you intend a roughly “fair fight”. Large monsters count as twice their hit dice, while small monsters count as half their hit dice.

In the real world, of course, fights are rarely fair – feel free to create monster encounters procedurally, and then allow the party a relevant task check to see if they notice which side has the upper hand.

### Monster Powers

Many monsters have various ‘budgeted’ powers, which they can activate a certain number of times per encounter. Monsters with higher hit dice will typically have more uses of their per-encounter powers; typical monsters are able to use their powers once per hd. Some monsters might have powers that cost two or even three “power points” per use, such as a magic-using monster that can cast second- or third-circle spells.

### Monster Behavior

Most monsters will have a default behavior, which is usually determined by rolling a d6. The possible behaviors for unintelligent monsters are fearful, wary, indifferent, curious, and aggressive. Fearful monsters will attempt to escape combat as soon as possible, and their save vs. fear rolls become harder once they become injured. Aggressive monsters will attempt to attack whenever possible, and their saves vs. fear when injured are easier. Monsters with the frenzy ability automatically succeed at their saving throws vs. fear, becoming enraged instead.

## Looting and Harvesting

Certain NPCs will have magic items that can be taken from them when they are defeated, and elemental or fey monsters will typically leave a corpse that can be harvested for materia. Any loot gathered from a monster or NPC should be scaled to that monster or NPC’s power level (represented by their hit dice). Thus, a  2 hd NPC will typically have a Common magic item, while a 3-4 hd NPC will typically have two common items or one uncommon item, and a 5 hd NPC might have three common, or one common and one uncommon, or one rare item.

It is possible, of course, for a 1 hit die NPC to be in the possession of a rare item or even a priceless artifact, but such circumstances don’t typically last very long. A 1 hit die NPC bristling with rare items might put up quite a fight at first, but their lower saving throws and hit points mean that they will eventually run out of luck.

Magical monsters will typically provide raw materials for crafting, rather than finished magic items, although exceptions are certainly possible (such as a magic sword embedded in the hide of a basilisk, or an entire cache of undigested weapons stuck inside of a gelatinous ooze). The most common treasure that can be harvested from a magical monster is raw materia; any monster with elemental powers will usually yield 1d6 charges of elemental materia per level when properly harvested. Harvesting materia from a monster is either a Survival check or a Science check; use whichever talent has the better threshold.

## Designing Your Own Monsters

A monster’s basic threat level is measured by its hit dice, but a referee should strive to make each of their monsters memorable. Always start with an idea that you find interesting, and then work from there towards something that fits into the hit die rating system.

### Hit Points

A monster’s hit points are determined by its Constitution, hit dice, and size. A mediumsized, 1 hit die monster has hit points equal to 6 + its Constitution, while a monster with two or more hit dice rolls all of its hit dice together (adding its Constitution to each die, to a minimum of 1 per roll) and adds the results to determine its base hit points.

Small creatures have half the hit points per hit die of medium creatures — compute their hit points as normal, and then halve that value (round up). Large creatures, on the other hand, have twice the hit points per hit die as medium creatures — again, compute their hit points as normal, and then double that value to determine maximum hit points. Huge monsters have roughly five times the hit points per hit die as medium creatures.

### Structure Points

Vehicles, constructs, and monsters such as dragons with magically-reinforced scale plating might have structure points in addition to or instead of hit points. One structure point is approximately equivalent to 5 hit points, and if a creature has both hit points and structure, all of its structure must be depleted before any hit point damage can be dealt, unless a critical hit is scored.

When an attack that normally deals hit point damage hits a structure, each damage die that rolls a '6' inflicts one structure point. If an attack that normally deals structure damage hits a creature or object with hit points, each D6 of structure damage becomes 5d6 hit point damage, and each +1 structure damage becomes +1d6 hit point damage.

### Vehicles and Constructs

Vehicles and constructs should generally be entirely made of structure points rather than hit points, and any ‘critical hit’ against them should use some kind of table to determine what part of the vehicle or construct is damaged. The golem armor rules are a good example of this; just about any vehicle could be made using the golem armor crafting system as a starting point.

### Giant Monsters

If a monster is Huge sized or larger, and has 100 or more hit points, consider splitting it up into multiple “sections”, each with its own hit points. Assign each of these sections to a number on a die, with larger sections being assigned multiple die results; each section gets one ‘share’ of hit points based on the number of die results it takes up. Consider giving each section an effect that happens when its hp are reduced to zero, and have one “main body”

section that receives spillover damage.

For example, a 100 hp ogre could have five sections out of a d20 hit location roll - body (18), legs (9-12), left arm (12-15) right arm (1619), and head (20). Each ‘roll’ would have 5 hit points; since the body takes up eight d20 ‘positions’, it has 40 hp; since the legs and each arm takes up four d20 ‘positions’, each limb (or set of limbs) has 20 hp. The head has 5 hp, since it only takes up a single d20 ‘position’. Whenever a hit lands against the ogre, the player rolls a d20 to determine which pool of hit points is depleted - or picks a section, if they rolled a hit with two d20s on an ‘easy’ attack roll.

If you use a d20 for the hit location roll, then you can make computing this split easier for yourself or other referees by halving the monster’s base hit dice and using this “base hp” with a multiplier for each section. Since a huge monster’s hp is 5x a medium creature, halving this gives you a “base hit points” of one-tenth the total; by multiplying this value by the number of hit chances on the d20 and then dividing by 2, you can quickly allocate its total hit points with a minimal margin of error.

### Monster Attacks

Monsters should deal damage that makes sense based on their size and shape. A creature with fangs like daggers should have a dagger-like attack, while a creature with claws like shortswords should have a shortsword-like attack. Large creatures deal double damage per attack, while Huge creatures deal five times their rolled damage. If a creature wields actual weapons with its hands, always use the attack statistics for that kind of weapon, adding more damage dice if the creature is large and wielding a weapon appropriate to its size.

A monster should be able to deal 5 points of damage per challenge rating on an average hit. You can adjust this upwards or downwards somewhat, but for every 5 full points of damage that a monster can consistently land above its challenge rating, consider reducing its total hit points by 15. Likewise, if a monster deals remarkably low damage, consider raising its hit points accordingly. Make this last choice sparingly; low-damage, high-hp “sponge” monsters are notoriously boring to fight.

Most monsters with a roughly animal body plan should have two ‘natural’ attacks - often one ‘bite’ attack and one ‘claw’ attack. These attacks should have slightly different accuracies and slightly different damage probabilities, with the higher-damage attack usually also being the lower-accuracy one.

Monsters with five hit dice or more should usually also have one ‘multiattack’, where they use all their natural attacks at once against a single target.

### Special Powers

Supernatural creatures with three or more hit dice should have a ‘special power’, either an attack or a spell-like ability that gives them tactical options in an encounter. Supernatural creatures with five or more hit dice should have two such powers, and creatures with nine or more hit dice should have three - with one of those three powers being significantly more potent than the other two. Feel free to assign powers based directly off of Wizard, Druid, or Priest powers, re-flavoring them as necessary to match the desired theme.

Non-supernatural creatures that are 3 hit dice or higher can also have a ‘special power’, but that power shouldn’t be particularly supernaturally “flavored”. Generally, creatures below human intelligence should have more Champion- or Hunter-like powers - but there will certainly be exceptions.

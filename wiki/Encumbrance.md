## Carrying Capacity

A character can only carry so much gear before they begin to be slowed and fatigued by it. A character’s maximum carrying capacity is equal to 6 + their Strength ability. Weight is tracked through "encumbrance"

units, which measure approximately how much someone is burdened by a particular piece of equipment. One encumbrance unit is usually about 15 lbs, but large or unwieldy items might encumber more than their weight would suggest.

When a character’s total carried weight exceeds half their carrying capacity, they are encumbered. This reduces their dash speed from 20 total paces per round to 10.

If a character's total carried weight exceeds their capacity, they are burdened. A burdened creature cannot dash, and their actions are always slow; if they try to carry more weight than their carrying capacity for more consecutive exploration turns than their Constitution, they will suffer 1d6 damage per subsequent exploration turn until they rest or lighten the load.

### Armor

Light armor adds 1 to encumbrance, medium armor adds 2, and heavy armor adds 3.

### Weapons

Light or medium weapons, tools, or potions at the ready do not count against encumbrance.

A heavy weapon counts as one encumbrance point, as does a shield or a similarly-sized tool. A particularly large heavy weapon or tool such as a greatbow, polearm, or ladder counts as two encumbrance points.

### Other Gear

A properly-worn and packed backpack counts as 1 encumbrance point for every 25 lb that it carries, while a similarly-sized sack roughly carried or slung over the back counts double. Large creatures only gain one encumbrance point per 100 lbs, and huge creatures only gain one encumbrance point per 500 lbs. A quadrupedal creature, such as a horse, doubles the pack weight that it can carry per encumbrance point for its size.

## Damage and Repair

Sundering weapons can damage armor, shields, and weapons; the referee might also decide that a particularly brutal critical hit causes similar damage. Damage to armor reduces its defense, while damage to a shield or weapon renders it useless until repaired. Armor reduces its effectiveness by 1 per ‘6’ rolled by the sundering weapon. Magical armor and weapons are typically made from feysilver or orichalc, and are much harder to damage — magical gear can only be damaged by a weapon of higher rarity.

Sundering damage might reduce an armor or weapon badly enough that it becomes useless, in which case the wearer is advised to drop it or take it off at the earliest convenience.

Weapons can simply be dropped, while shields take a combat action to remove.

Removing armor in combat requires a number of successful Finesse checks equal to the armor’s type – one for light armor, two for medium, and three for heavy – with each Finesse check taking a full action.

Damaged metal armor or weapons must be repaired by a blacksmith with access to a forge. Preparing a forge for work takes an hour of effort (six exploration turns), plus fuel for the fire; a trained blacksmith can attempt to halve this time (to 3 turns) with a Finesse roll – success readies the forge, while failure wastes the half hour of effort. Once the forge is hot and the workspace is prepared, each -1 reduction in defense requires one turn of effort and a Finesse test. Success repairs 1 point of defense, up to the armor’s original integrity. A weapon or shield usually only takes one turn of effort to repair.

Other damaged gear might be repairable or not, depending on the item. If the referee decides that an item is repairable, someone with suitable skill and tools can make a Finesse roll as their exploration action to attempt to repair one -1 reduction. Conditions and quality of tools will make this roll easier or harder, based on the referee’s judgment.

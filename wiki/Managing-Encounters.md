## Managing Encounters

## Starting an Encounter

Generally, an encounter starts whenever one creature initiates hostilities against another, or whenever a trap or hazard activates that requires rapid reactions and decisions. In an encounter, seconds count, and the party is fighting against the clock as much as they are fighting against the dungeon or its monsters. An encounter starts with a surprise check. If either side was moving stealthily, all creatures on that side roll Stealth. Then, each creature that was on watch on either side makes an Alertness check. (Usually, this happens in the turn just before the encounter begins.)

Creatures who were performing complex or deeply engrossing exploration actions like preparing a spell, repairing an object, or meditating to restore glamour or faith are fully surprised during the first round of combat, and begin the encounter stunned; they must skip the first round of combat.

Any other creature declares its first action, and then all creatures that can act do so in order based on the speed of their actions.

Action speed determines the order that each creature or hazard acts during a six-second combat round. All fast actions go first, followed by all normal actions, and then all slow actions. If two creatures have the same action speed, they act simultaneously unless the precise timing matters, in which case they each make a Dexterity check and the higher result chooses whether to go first or last.

Each creature performs its movement and action during the appropriate phase of the round. Other creatures who  may react to the acting creature, if the acting creature’s action affects the reacting creature or provokes an attack from the reacting creature.

The currently acting creature can choose to delay their action, if they want to do something directly in response to the action of a slower creature or hazard. They describe the condition they are waiting for and their desired response to it, then wait. When the specified condition occurs, they can interrupt that action with their own to perform the desired response, which may include movement.

Once all creatures that could perform actions have acted, and all creatures that couldn't perform actions have handled any reflexive bookkeeping, the round ends and the next round begins.

## Surprise and Ambushes

Certain situations can cause surprise if one side deliberately sets up to ambush the other. The player characters’ party is just as capable of ambushing a goblin warband as they are of being ambushed by them.

Even if a party is successfully ambushed, most characters still have some chance to act during the surprise round, which is the first round of a combat encounter when at least some creatures are surprised. Any creature who was on watch and succeeded at their Alertness check gets to act in the surprise round, while any other creature is stunned and must wait until the surprise round is over.

## Morale

Most creatures will attempt to retreat from a fight if injured (reduced below half their maximum hit points). An injured monster or NPC must immediately make a saving throw vs. Fear to determine whether they stay in the fight, and must make another saving throw each time they sustain further damage while they are below half their hit points. On a failure, that creature will panic and attempt to flee. Some creatures are immune to morale checks, or have other behaviors that they will perform instead of fleeing.

Player characters are assumed to be made of sterner stuff than this, and will not need to make morale checks unless subjected to some kind of supernatural fear-inducing effect. If you and your players want to play a grittier game, you can require player characters to make morale checks in any circumstance that an NPC would need to make them; a player character that fails a morale check will be limited in which actions they can take, but you should still allow them to decide how their character tries to flee.

## Movement in Combat

Characters in combat can walk, sprint, jump, climb, or fly - sometimes all at once! Each mode of movement deserves special consideration.

For simplicity’s sake, nearly all creatures have a base movement speed of 5 paces, or 3 paces if hobbled. A character can dash to quadruple that to 20 paces if unencumbered, or merely double it to 10 paces if encumbered. Difficult terrain also reduces a character’s total speed for the round to 3 paces (as if hobbled).

Jumping, climbing, and swimming require the character to spend their action and make an Agility test; the difficulty (and consequences of failure) should be based on the nature of the terrain being crossed.

## Opportunity Attacks

One situation that you’ll need to adjudicate frequently is the opportunity attack. For a high lethality game, most actions that a player character, NPC, or monster could take should trigger an opportunity attack, unless that action is itself a melee attack, the guard action, or the evade action.

If you want a lower-lethality game, you might restrict opportunity attacks to only trigger when a creature attempts to leave a hostile creature’s reach, or attempts to make a ranged attack or cast a ranged spell.

An interesting compromise is to allow fighter classes an opportunity attack on any action other than guard, evade, or melee attacks, while non-warriors only gain opportunity vs. creatures leaving their reach or attacking at range.

## Ongoing Conditions

Various kinds of ongoing conditions can be inflicted on a creature due to damage. The most common source of these conditions is when an attack's damage die rolls a '6'.

Weapon attacks will usually inflict bleeding, stun, or sundering damage, while elemental attacks will inflict conditions based on their element. A creature gets a saving throw to avoid any ongoing condition inflicted by an attack, and some conditions allow a creature to spend their action to make another saving throw to attempt to end them.

Petrification and cold attacks will usually hobble a creature first, then restrain them if they are already hobbled, then immobilize them if they are already restrained. Hobbled and restrained creatures can attempt to break free with a saving throw, but an immobilized creature can only be freed with outside help. Sharp weapons, acid, poison, and fire will all inflict the ongoing damage effect, which is applied at the end of each round of combat until the creature takes an action to deal with the condition, and makes a successful saving throw or task check, depending on how the player describes their action.

Radiant and thunder damage can blind or deafen a creature, which lasts until the affliction is magically removed; resting and recovering hit points might allow a saving throw vs. Doom to recover a temporary condition at the referee's discretion.

Blunt weapons, elemental lightning, and psychic damage usually stun a creature, although psychic attacks can also cause various curse or beguiling effects. These effects will usually be unique enough that they must be handled on a case-by-case basis. Finally, aetheric damage represents any purely magical attack with no mundane equivalent; any ongoing effect from aetheric damage will need to be handled on a case-bycase basis.

## Adjusting Difficulty

Combat encounters are one of the places where referee’s rulings to adjust difficulty are trickiest, and most important. Players will want to try all sorts of things to try to make their attacks hit harder or land more easily. Your job is to encourage this without breaking verisimilitude or bogging down play.

In terms of statistical probabilities, remember that easy and hard difficulties do not change the maximum or minimum possible rolls that a character could achieve for a given task – they simply move the ‘middle’ of their dice roll probabilities upwards or downwards, making average rolls more or less likely to achieve the upper end of their potential.

### Cover and Concealment

Cover and concealment are two of the more common adjustments to attack difficulty. Both cover and concealment can be partial or total. Partial concealment means that at least half of a character’s silhouette is obscured, or that their form is indistinct. It might also mean that they are only visible for brief moments during the combat round. Dim light counts as partial concealment, as do certain illusion magics such as the blur spell. Partial concealment will make ranged attacks harder, and prevent most melee attacks from being made easier.

Full concealment means that the target is effectively invisible to the attacker. This makes melee attacks harder, and ranged attacks impossible unless they have some other way of precisely guiding their attack to the target. Full concealment also makes it easier for a concealed creature to attack anyone that cannot see them.

Partial cover makes all attacks against the covered creature harder (just like a shield), and makes most of their saving throws easier. Full cover, on the other hand, is one of the few things that can make an attack impossible, but this requires that the creature is fully protected by cover for the entire round.

## Crazy Stunts

The players need to be rewarded for creativity in combat, but rewards without risk will quickly make for a dull adventure. If a player wants their character to try some kind of stunt in combat that isn’t directly handled by the rules but seems vaguely plausible, by all means let them - figure out a talent or ability and difficulty, and have them roll. Remember that task checks performed during encounters only have six seconds or less to complete, so the kinds of actions that can be performed in an encounter should be much more limited than those that can be performed during a full ten-minute exploration turn.

### Defensive Stunts

Dodging and parrying are the two most common stunts used to defend against an attack, but sometimes the players will come up with a clever idea that isn’t really either. Other defensive stunts can be handled as saving throws or task checks; just have the target make a saving throw or roll whatever talent or ability seems appropriate to determine whether it succeeds.

### Attack Stunts

Stunts performed as part of an attack need to square up against the standard attack rules. If the player has come up with an idea that genuinely isn’t handled by the rules, use your judgment to determine whether the stunt should make their attack easier or harder, make their opponent’s saving throw harder or easier, or cause some other effect - such as knocking the opponent down or knocking them back, stunning them, bypassing their armor, or distracting them enough to allow an ally an immediate opportunity attack. The key is to make sure the players feel rewarded for their ingenuity, but aren’t being given an ‘easy win’ just for thinking of something new. Often, you can use an attack's damage roll to decide what happens - a special effect might happen if one or more damage dice roll '6'; if a talent seems relevant to the attempt, you can compare the attacker’s talent threshold to the attack's highest or lowest damage die, as if the damage roll was a task check: if the attack hits and the "task check" succeeds, the additional effect happens. (The target might get a saving throw depending on the effect.)

### Improvised Magic

Stunts performed as part of a spell or miracle can be the most challenging, and the most fun, situations to rule on. It helps to have a reasonable understanding of real-world science, a reasonable sense of why the game’s magic systems work the way they do, a fair grounding in mythology and fairy-tales, and a good imagination when going “off the rails”

with magical effects: the situation should ‘feel’ physically and magically appropriate to the situation. Feel free to push back if you don’t feel like you can accommodate the stunt; magic is fickle and unpredictable, and “you try it but it just doesn’t work” is a fine response to trying something  weird with magic.

A good rule of thumb here, as everywhere, is to balance risk against reward - a good idea on the part of a clever player should let them take a moderate risk with a substantial payoff, while a bad idea that they still really want to try should be high-risk, with an appropriate reward if the die rolls work out in their favor. Remember that as the referee, your job is to be a fair adjudicator of the rules and the situation at hand; let the players do what they want to do, then use their actions and the result of the dice to decide fairly how to describe the consequences of their choices.

### Magical Mishaps

With magic, especially, “trying something weird” should give you as the referee an opportunity for creative exploration. Magic is notoriously tricky and seems to delight in ironic outcomes – a player attempting something strange might get exactly what they were asking for, only to discover that “exactly what they asked for” has unforeseen but annoyingly predictable consequences.

In other situations, a player attempting an unusual magical effect might get the opposite of what they wanted, or might even cause a seemingly unrelated phenomenon to manifest - there is a long-standing tradition of rolling "wild magic" mishap effects in the fantasy role-playing genre.

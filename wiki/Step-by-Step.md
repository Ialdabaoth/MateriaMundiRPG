## H. Step-by-Step:

## Travel

Each one-league hex of wilderness travel usually takes approximately one hour, using the following procedure.

#### Step 0 - Describe the Road Ahead

Decide what landmarks are visible and describe them to the party, along with the weather, signs of life, and any other relevant features.

#### Step 1 - Stealth or Speed

The party first decides whether they are going to move normally, stealthily, or attempt to force march through a hex. If they choose Stealth, have each character immediately make a Stealth check and note if anyone fails; if they choose to force march, each character makes a save vs. Doom to fend off fatigue.

#### Step 2 - Assign Roles

Have the party decide who is going to navigate, who is going to keep watch, and who is going to tend to any animals and gear.

#### Step 3 - Task Checks

Each member of the party makes a task check appropriate to their assigned role:

- Navigators roll Survival

- Guards roll Alertness

- Other characters roll any talent or ability

that the referee deems appropriate.

#### Step 4 - Encounter Check

Make a Random Encounter roll for the new hex that the party is entering. If there is no random encounter, move on to the next league of travel and repeat this process. If there is, determine what appears and choose an appropriate encounter map.

#### Step 4a - Surprise Check

If either party was successfully moving stealthily, then they will automatically surprise the other party that they have encountered. If at least one member of a party failed their Stealth check, though, then the other party has an opportunity to see them coming if any of their guards succeeded at their Alertness checks.

## J. Step-by-Step:

## Traps and Locks

Dungeon traps, locked doors, and hazards need to be detected, inspected, and disarmed.

#### Step 1 - Detecting the Trap or Lock

Each player that is actively looking for traps or secret doors rolls an Alertness check, rolling two d6’s. Treat the two d6’s as an easy roll if the player is looking in the right place or in the right way, or as a hard roll if they aren’t.

#### Step 1a - Inspecting the Mechanism

Once a trap has been detected, an inspecting player can roll their Science vs. the lock or trap’s inspect difficulty (easy, normal, or hard). If they fail, they are mistaken about the lock or trap’s mechanism; if they succeed, then they understand the basics of how it works. Various specific ideas for how to inspect the trap might make the trap’s Inspect difficulty easier or harder. If the Science check fails, the inspecting player must make a Finesse check to avoid setting the trap off with the inspection attempt.

#### Step 2 - Disarming

A player who thinks they understand how the trap or lock works can attempt to pick or disarm it with a Finesse check. If their Finesse check fails, or if they describe their attempt to disarm a trap in a way that's likely to set it off, the referee might call for a second Finesse check to see if the trap goes off.

#### Step 3 - Activation

If a trap activates, it will usually perform an attack – often an area attack, but sometimes a single-target ranged or melee attack vs. the person who activated the trap. If the trap has a more elaborate mechanism that operates over multiple combat rounds, then an encounter begins.

## K. Step-by-Step:

## Combat

In 99% of cases, arbitrating combat should be straightforward and intuitive. When things get complicated - usually due to multiple actions “stacking up” on top of each other - the referee should slow down and walk through the whole round step-by-step.

#### Step 0 - Declare Actions

At the start of the round, each creature that can act declares its action and determines the speed of that action. Each creature that is attacking declares what weapon they are attacking with, but doesn't have to declare who they are attacking yet. Each creature that is attempting to perform magic must declare what spell, miracle, glamour, or other effect it intends to cast.

#### Step 0a - Determine Speed

Each weapon attack uses that weapon's size to determine its speed; claws and other innate melee attacks are always fast, and breath weapons and the like are always slow. Magic is always slow, and evading and guarding are always fast.

#### Step 0b - Resolve Conflicts

If there are any conflicts in the order of events because two different creatures each want to see what the other one wants to do before deciding what they want to do, whichever creature is lower level declares its actions first. If both creatures are the same level, then neither can declare their action in response to the other.

#### Step 1 - Fast Actions

All fast movement and attacks happen first. If two creatures both have a fast action and the order of their actions matters, they both make a Dexterity check, with the higher roll choosing whether to go first or second.

#### Step 1a - Movement

An attacking creature that can also move can decide how much of its movement to perform before or after its attack.

#### Step 1b - Opportunity Attacks

If at any time it attempts to move away from a creature that could make an opportunity attack against it, that creature can interrupt its movement to make its attack; if it hits, the interrupted creature loses the rest of its movement and any other actions it hasn't taken yet. The opportunity attacker uses its action for the round making the opportunity attack, unless it was guarding.

Similarly, if a creature tries to make a ranged attack while another creature could make an opportunity attack against it, that creature can interrupt its attack with its own attack.

#### Step 2 - Normal Actions

All normal actions and movement happen next, with the same sub-steps as fast actions.

#### Step 3 - Slow Actions

All slow actions and movement happen next, with the same sub-steps as fast actions and normal actions.

#### Step 4 - Bookkeeping

Any end-of-round bookkeeping happens after any slow actions, such as saving throws, ongoing damage, and the like. Then, if there are still any active combatants, the next round of combat begins.

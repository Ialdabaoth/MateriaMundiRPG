## Purchasing Gear

Player characters have to get their stuff from somewhere.

A player character is assumed to have purchased their starting equipment before play begins; if the referee desires, they may instead give each player 2d6 x 10 silver to purchase all their equipment with.

### Prices and Markets

The costs listed here are simply a rough estimate of what an average adventurer could expect to be charged by an average shopkeep. Prices should vary according to the local economy and the availability of specific goods. A good rule of thumb is to look at the modern cost of an equivalent good or service in US dollars or Euros, and then multiply by 100 to roughly gauge its cost in drakhs.

These costs are listed in silver pieces; across the Successor Kingdoms, 1 silver piece equals 10 copper, and 1 gold piece is 10 silver. These costs are roughly standard, but might adjust up or down by a factor of 2 depending on the local economic conditions. Custom-made gear will be, on average, two to five times more expensive than something bought “off the shelf”, but will be higher quality. If in doubt, use a multiplier of (2d6+3) x 10% for an offthe-shelf item, or (3d6 x 25%) for a piece of custom work. If the player wants to haggle, you can apply a price adjustment in the winner’s favor after a Wit or Charm contest of somewhere between 10% and 25%, depending on how the haggling plays out.

For basic weapons and armor, the prices listed here are for a common version; masterwork items can fetch up to ten times the listed price. Such weapons may be readily available for sale at market, or they may need to be commissioned in advance from the local blacksmith. The availability of most other items and services, especially magic items, will depend greatly on the size and prosperity of the city; small hamlets are unlikely to have rare magic items for sale, and if they do, there will undoubtedly be an interesting story behind why.

## Selling Gear

Player characters can also attempt to sell their gear to interested parties. The selling price will usually start at around half the listed buying price; the potential buyer's attitude will adjust this up or down by up to 25%.

From that point, it's up to the players to haggle, with the referee playing the part of the buyer. Intuition, Bearing, Charm, and Wit task checks will all come into play.

If the referee is rewarding extra experience from treasure accumulated, selling gear or treasure to an interested buyer does not count towards experience gained from finding treasure.  Any gear found in the dungeon will award experience based on its full purchase price, at which point the party can choose to convert it into more liquid assets if they wish.

### Stealing from Shops

It's inevitable that someone is going to want to steal from the local shopkeep. Try to remind the players that this isn't generally that kind of game; if there is a priest among the player characters, they should be given the first chance to handle the situation before the referee steps in. That said, sometimes extenuating circumstances do prevail.

Attempting to shoplift is a Stealth roll, while the shopkeep and any nearby guards make an Alertness check. Only if the Stealth check succeeds and everyone's Alertness check fails does the thief get away with it. This Stealth check is easier for Scout characters, who are well-trained in all sorts of sleight-of-hand. Most local baronies do not look kindly on thieves, and stealing anything of value is likely to bring an adventurer's career to an abrupt end. Stealing back from those who are already outside the protection of the law is another matter, of course, but such people rarely have welcoming storefronts in wellpopulated villages protected by a feudal lord.

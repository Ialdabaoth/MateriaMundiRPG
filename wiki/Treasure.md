## Treasure

One of the main lures of adventuring in dungeons is the lucrative trade in looted treasures. Dungeon treasure that the player does not have a use for can be converted into gold, silver, and copper coins, which can be used to purchase other goods and services.

Sometimes, dungeon treasure will even come in the form of chests or bags of loose coins, ready to be spent. Otherwise, selling treasure requires finding a buyer and haggling.

### Experience for Treasure

Defeating challenges and gaining treasure are how adventurers increase in level. If the party survives an adventure, makes it back into town, and cashes in their acquired loot, they gain experience if the referee determines that they have met the criteria for an experience milestone.

### Currency

Most kingdoms coin currency based on a standard inherited from the Old Empire. The standard large denomination is a one-ounce gold coin, called the drakhma or drac. A drac typically has a serpentine celestial dragon on one side, wound around a polearm bundled with staves; most merchants will abbreviate this symbol as an ‘S’ with a vertical line driven through it. The obverse side will usually be stamped with a profile portrait of the monarch that commissioned the coinage.

The next smaller unit of currency is the halfounce silver denarius or dime, with ten dimes equaling one drac. A dime typically has a cornucopia on one side, and a stamped threequarters profile of a baron or duke who happened to have the monarch’s favor at the time of printing. One dime is traditionally equal to one day’s wages for an unskilled laborer, and in fact the Church sets the minimum wage for any daily laborer at one denarius, whether free or slave.

Finally, the smallest unit of currency is the half-ounce copper penny, with ten pennies equaling one dime. A penny usually has a laurel wreath on one side, and the portrait of a victorious knight on the other. The knight’s heraldry will usually be stamped within the circle of the laurel wreath. Many knights aspire to earn their laurels, and have their deeds commemorated on the face of a coin.

### Weight and Purity

The drac, dime, and penny are all typically stamped to be exactly one inch in diameter, with the thickness varying to achieve the correct precious metal weight. A proper drac will typically contain 90% gold by weight, alloyed with silver to harden the metal so that it can remain in circulation for longer. Dimes are likewise typically 90% silver and 10% copper, while pennies are 95% copper alloyed with a variable mixture of tin and zinc.

### Alternate Currencies

Dwarves mint their own currency, which they pragmatically named the copper, nickel, and quarter for their component metals (quarters are 25% gold and 75% silver). One copper is equivalent to a penny, while a nickel is worth five coppers and a quarter is worth five nickels. Dwarven mints are honest to a fault, so merchants in the Successor Kingdoms typically accept dwarven currency without much hassle.

While larger denominations than the drac exist, they are typically only used to transfer wealth between kingdoms or principalities.

Most such wealth is transferred directly using bullion bars of gold, gemstones, or even feysilver or orichalc.

### Merchants and Artisans

Many civilized settlements and traveling merchants will offer goods and services that the party can purchase. Each purveyor of fine goods exists within a community, and has their own sense of the local market. As such, the price of goods can vary wildly from region to region, based on supply and demand.

A good rule of thumb is that a skilled merchant or artisan expects to be paid for the time and skill put into the objects they are creating or transporting. Above that, merchants will sell their goods at prevailing market price, which can be quite different than the price of the materials and time needed to create those goods. Taxes and safety also take their cut, driving merchants’ prices up higher - a merchant must either pay the local government to protect their wares, pay mercenaries to protect them directly, or protect those wares themselves with their own time and martial prowess, all of which cut into the bottom line.

### Lifestyle Costs

The price of shelter, food, and safety can vary significantly based on their quality, from the meager to the extravagant. The average adventure ranges the gamut of highs and lows, based on their fortune from day to day. A typically healthy and well-cooked but not extravagant meal will often range from 2 to 3 copper at a traveler’s inn, while a night’s stay might cost a dime or more. Ingredients for a home-cooked meal will typically cost about a copper, and a month’s rent of a cottage in a reasonably safe town or village might cost between two and five gold.

These prices will fluctuate wildly depending on economic conditions, of course; a boom town that grows around a newly-discovered goldmine might have prices up to a hundred times more expensive, while a famine-starved region might well treat grain as more precious than gold.

Adventurers who are lucky enough to own their own property still have to contend with taxes, repair and maintenance on their homes, and other cost-sinks. Almost every property worth owning will exist under the protection of a feudal lord, who will expect to be paid the halftithe for the service of keeping the lands free of bandits (regardless of how well or poorly they do that job). Properties that are not under the aegis of a feudal protector will need their own protection from brigands, burglars and bugbears that might want to raid the larder or even move in and declare the place their own.

Minions and hirelings also add to lifestyle costs, since they expect to be paid in exchange for the services they provide. Minions and hirelings that are “on retainer” will need to be paid whether or not they are accompanying an adventure (although they will obviously expect to be paid more when in the line of danger); a retainer of trained warriors is a useful way to keep the party’s home turf secure and protected while they are away on their adventures.

### Charity and Reputation

Treasure can also simply be given away. If the party is noticeably generous in a particular region, they might develop a reputation for their wealth and generosity. This comes with both upsides and downsides; commoners and merchants are likely to be friendlier and more willing to render aid, but bandits and taxcollectors will likely see generous adventurers as particularly rich targets for theft, extortion, or other forms of harassment.

Priests who give their treasure away towards divinely-sanctioned charity will quickly gain the attention of the Church, and might be fasttracked into leadership roles as a result. The Church values competence in its leadership, so a player character cannot simply "buy their way" into a cushy Cardinalship without being able to actually attend to all the duties that the position will entail. Still, generosity and luck go a long way towards gaining the Church's favor, especially when that generosity clearly results in improving the lives of the people. Another way to use treasure to cultivate a reputation is to spend it on ostentatious displays of upper-class finery.  Often, a statusminded party will split its spending evenly between lavish displays of personal wealth, and genuine public works that improve the lives of the citizenry. Money spent on display is not truly wasted; a kingdom that proudly flaunts its prosperity with beautiful public art and architecture is also telling its neighbors that it is well-funded enough to be able to easily defend itself from invasion.

### Domain Construction and Management

The party can also spend their treasure on creating a domain for themselves, building a keep (or more likely, hiring architects and masons to build it for them) and cultivating the surrounding land. The full rules for domain construction and management are in the Referee's Guide, as appendix F.

Player characters who come to rule a domain will also need to defend it, which will entail hiring and training an army. Taxation and military armament are detailed in appendix F, while the rules for actually deploying armies, siege engines, and other weapons of war into mass combat are detailed in appendix G.

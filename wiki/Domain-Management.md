## F. Domain Management

The medieval world is dominated by feudal societies. In feudalism, the land of a kingdom is held by a king or queen, who grants noble titles to those who pledge to defend it. A noble title corresponds to a portion of land, called a fief, which that noble is expected to defend - so a county is given to a count, a barony to a baron, a duchy to a duke, a bordermarch to a marquis, and so on. By 5th level, an adventurer will have made enough of a name for themselves that they might plausibly be granted a title of nobility, a royally recognized coat-of-arms for their heraldry, and a small fiefdom to rule.

Alternatively, barbarians or outlaws might have amassed enough power that they can simply take control of an area by force, acting as its de facto rulers if not recognized de jure by the king.

### Noble Rank

The lowest title of nobility is the knight.

Anyone who shows particular merit on the battlefield can be knighted by a king, regardless of their station of birth; upon knighthood, they enter into the nobility and all its attendant expectations. Knighthood is not directly hereditary, but most knights train their sons to follow in their footsteps, so it is customary for the crown to grant a son their father’s title when the time comes.

Above the knight in rank is the baron or marquis. These titles are typically hereditary, passing down from father to son, although a particularly loyal and skilled knight might be granted a barony or bordermarch if one becomes available. The distinction between baron and marquis is chiefly one of geography and expected duty; a bordermarch is simply a martial barony on the border of two potentially hostile kingdoms, and so a marquis can expect to see more trouble than a comparatively peaceful baron. Above the barons and marquis are the counts, and above the counts are the dukes, and above the dukes are the king or queen - the true sovereign of the entire kingdom.

### Oath of Fealty

A newly-minted noble will enter into service by immediately swearing an oath of fealty to their sovereign, typically sanctified directly by an attending priest. Various pagan kingdoms might swear a geas before a druid instead, binding the health of the lord directly to the health of the land – and vice versa.

### Noble Privileges

A feudal vassal is only answerable to their lord and the Church; their lord, in turn, is only answerable to their lord, with the Successor Kings only truly answerable to the High Father himself. Knights are expected to uphold their barons’ laws and keep the peace within their fiefdoms; their baron in turn is expected to answer to their duke and ultimately to their prince or king. The church takes matters of abuse of power quite seriously, but still grants secular rulers a good deal of latitude in ruling over their subjects. Knights are also granted a small fief of land from their baron or marquis, from which they are expected to collect the halftithe that all peasants and vassals pay to their immediate superiors. A questing knight that comes upon treasure can keep half of what they find or take by conquest, paying the remainder to their duke as part of their halftithe.

While most of the taxes collected from the halftithe go towards defense and welfare of the realm, dukes and counts and princes still tend to find a way to enrich themselves substantially. Signs of luxury are signs of prosperity, after all, and it is unwise for a ruler to appear weak to his neighbors.

Knights, similarly, are seen as a reflection of their rulers, so particularly wealthy barons will expect their knights to dress and comport themselves appropriately so as to avoid besmirching their lord with a beggarly reputation in the sight of his peers.

### Noble Responsibilities

The ruler of a domain and their knight retainers have many obligations to their subjects, beyond the basic military defense of the domain. The Church maintains its own formal hierarchy, separate from the nobility, in order to keep watch over kings and their lieutenants and confirm that they are fulfilling their sacred oaths.

#### Marriage and Succession

One of the greatest obligations that a ruler has to their subjects, according to the Church, is to reassure those subjects of the stability of their rule. A happy and prosperous kingdom, ruled over by a wise and benevolent ruler, can be brought down in short time by a despotic usurper or descendant. Further, as the chief duty of a noble lord is to take up arms and protect their subjects on the field of battle, a cowardly or weak ruler is obviously unworthy of their fiefdom.

To that end, it is the duty of every noble of marquis rank or higher to marry a wife of suitable skill and cunning, and designate a legitimate heir, and dutifully train that heir in the defense and administration of the domain. Each Successor Kingdom interprets this duty slightly differently. In some areas of Iberas and Gauhl, gender and breeding are seen somewhat inflexibly; a Lord must be a strong male of noble birth, and his Lady must be a trained courtesan from an allied noble house. Further north and west, these strictures are interpreted much more loosely – in most Northman kingdoms, the “Lord” of the fief is whichever of the married pair takes up the chief duty of defense, and the “Lady” is whichever has taken up the chief duty of clerical administration; same-sex pairings are reasonably common, and many heirs are produced through a concubine or acquired through adoption.

Regardless of the biological details, it is the duty of the Lord to defend the fiefdom in wartime, and it is the duty of the Lady to attend to and nurture its spiritual health in peacetime. To that end, a proper Lord will be some form of warrior class, and a proper Lady will be some form of magic-user, with various expert-class viziers and attendants to advise them both in matters of state.

#### Courts and Justice

As the ruler of a domain, a feudal noble is expected to preside over matters of law, and adjudicate disputes between subjects.

Typically, priests are appointed judicial duties for most minor matters, leaving the noble to focus on pressing matters of state. Crimes committed by or against entitled servants of the noble are themselves matters of state, and so are typically presided over personally by the noble and judged by a council of peers.

The Church holds certain principles of justice to be sacred, and so withholds communion from any feudal lord who denies these rights to their subjects.

**Right to Life -** all mortal subjects, regardless of station, have the right to live, and shall not be deprived of life, limb, or health by another except in immediate defense when in genuine mortal danger.

**Right to Freedom -** no mortal subject, regardless of station, shall be enslaved, imprisoned, or otherwise prevented from free travel or association for a span of more than seven years without their consent.

**Right to Justice -** A mortal subject aggrieved by another shall be entitled to restitution, and a mortal subject who transgresses against the law or his fellow shall be punished proportionate to the severity of the offense. **Right to Mercy -** A mortal subject shall have the right to repent of their transgressions against the law and their fellow, to be judged by the sincerity of their repentance and amends, and restored to communion.

**Right to Property -** A mortal subject shall have the right to the fruits of his own labor, subject only to those reasonable taxes due to the sovereign who protects these rights on his behalf, and that sovereign’s properly appointed servants. No taxation shall exceed the halftithe, except in dire need; and all taxes in excess of the halftithe shall be recompensed to those from whom they were taxed.

**Right to Trust -** Any mortal subject who gives their sworn word to another, witnessed before the law and the Church, shall be held to proper account for their promise by the Church and the Law.

### Domain Size

A knight will typically hold a fiefdom that is approximately 10 square miles in area - about the size of one league hex - although larger holdings might also contain up to the six surrounding hexes. A baron or marquis might control thirty such hexes in their own private demesne (the area of their domain that does not include the fiefs administered by the knights under their command), while a count might command a hundred or more.

### Building a Domain

A knight is expected to build a keep, or castle, in a position so that their entire fief can be seen from atop the castle’s towers; a baron is expected to be able to at least see the tops of the towers of their nearest vassal knights from the highest tower of their own keep.

Most keeps are built in a motte and bailey structure - the motte is the castle proper, where the knight and their retinue live and train. Surrounding the motte is a bailey of farmlands, houses, and shops, all living under the protection of the keep. In times of trouble, the subjects of the bailey can retreat to the motte, pull the motte’s draw-bridge, and try to wait out a raid or siege in relative safety. When a new fief is awarded to a newly-titled knight, the domain will often be completely uncultivated and unprotected. It will be the knight’s first duty to survey the land, break ground, build their keep, and attract subjects that will plough the fields, work the forges, tend to the shops, and so on. A knight and their keep are the basic unit of civilization, so the founding of a new keep is sure to attract those hoping for safety and stability.

Of equal importance to the keep itself is the chapel. While the knight tends to the physical safety of their subjects, the priest tends to their spiritual safety. Spiritual dangers are wellknown and well-feared, not least of which are the undead, the infernal, and the fair folk. A priest and his clergy will spend most of their time tending to the moral and psychological health of the knight’s subjects, but when unholy forces threaten their flock, their lord will call upon them to aid in the fight directly.

#### Gathering Supplies

Constructing a proper keep requires wood, stone, iron, and labor — but chief among these is labor.  Labor must be paid, or otherwise provided for; Church-enforced law and custom dictate that a laborer receive no less than one silver denarius per day of labor. This sets a cost per month of 3 gold per unskilled laborer.

Skilled laborers expect to be paid approximately twice this amount, or 6 gold per month, while true craftsmen, engineers, and overseers typically expect to be paid around 10 gold per month per experience level. A famous architect or artisan might regularly command twice this amount.

Haggling for a bargain with each laborer is more trouble than it’s worth, so most projects simply pay the going rate for anyone below the master architect.

#### Breaking Ground

The first step in building a keep is to clear the ground. Workers come and set up camp, and begin clearing stone, chopping down trees, pulling up their roots, and smoothing and compacting the soil. Stone, wood, and earth from the clearing are stockpiled for later construction, but the key at this stage is to create a solid, smooth, open foundation to build upon.

While the area is being cleared, the attending high priest will begin to ritually consecrate the ground. This involves performing the hallow ritual at each sunrise and sunset for a full lunar month, until the blessing ‘weaves into’ the earth itself and becomes permanent.

Once the ground is suitably blessed, and the foundations properly laid, craftsmen can begin constructing the keep itself. Most keeps are designed by a Science-trained architect, who uses their understanding of engineering to determine the ideal shape and materials for the walls, floors and ceilings.

The outer defenses of the motte come first, then the castle and chapel proper, and finally the supporting buildings. Peasant and freeman subjects will typically begin arriving at this time, both to help with construction and to help establish the bailey village that will support and maintain the new keep.

#### Designing The Keep

The keep, chapel, and motte walls are each split into various individual rooms and buildings, each of which can measure up to 100’ on a side. The material costs listed are per 5’ section of wall; locally gathered material instead costs labor wages equal to half its gp value. For each room, the referee should determine the quality of the materials gathered.

For each room, the architect rolls a Science check; this is made easier with high-quality materials and labor, or harder with lowquality supplies. On a success, each 5’ section of wall has full structure points. On a failure, multiply each wall section’s structure in that room by (1d6-1), then divide by 5.

At minimum, a proper keep requires a great hall to receive guests, a chapel to house religious services, quarters for the lord and guests to retire to, stables to tend to their horses, an armory to store weapons, and workshops to maintain them.

**The Great Hall** serves as the centerpiece of a keep, and can comfortably entertain one person of importance per 500 square feet of space. A well-designed great hall speaks to the majesty and wealth of its occupants – if the architect achieves an exceptional success during design and construction, and the facilities maintain the highest quality decorations and supplies, the hall is considered to be exceptional - making all Charm, Wit and Leadership checks that the lord or his lieutenants make easier while hosting within the halls.

**The Chapel** is where the clergy attends to their congregation. A chapel can comfortably host one person for every 50 square feet of space, and typically devotes extra space to private studies, a reliquary, and the living space of the clergy. Hundreds of treatises on the proper design of chapels have been written, emphasizing the effect of architecture on the mindset of those who worship within.

If the architect achieves an exceptional success during the design and construction of the chapel, and the clergy maintain its blessings and stock it with the highest quality decorations and supplies, the chapel is considered to be exceptional - making all Divine miracles, rituals, and saving throws performed within it easier.

A chapel will often contain an infirmary, where the sick or injured can rest and heal while attended to by priests and healers. An infirmary can comfortably house one patient for every 500 square feet of space, while giving their doctors room to move and work around them.

**Quarters -** A knight and his family typically take up 500 square feet of space per person. Additional spaces are also often devoted to guest rooms, trophy halls, dining rooms, and various other leisures and luxuries.

The lord’s retinue and men-at-arms will typically live within separate barracks structures, so that they can work and train together. Workers’ and soldiers’ quarters typically house one medium mortal for every 200 square feet of space, with a communal sleeping room, a separate communal washroom, a large kitchen, and a dining area and meeting hall. More luxurious quarters will offer up to 1000 square feet of space for nobility and their guests, with their own private washrooms and dining areas.

**Stable -** Domesticated animals require stables to house and train them. A stable can hold one Large animal or two Medium animals for every 200 square feet of space, so long as there is an equal amount of fenced open-air area attached to the stables for exercise, sunlight, and grazing. Stables will frequently have a second story to store feed and supplies, as well as a few small barracks-like quarters for the stable attendants to sleep.

<!-- p. 267 -->
**Armory -** An armory stores armor, weapons, ammunition, and other gear necessary for martial defense. Typically, every 25 square feet of space is sufficient to store enough gear to outfit one medium sized man-at-arms, and keep that gear organized and ready so that a soldier can outfit themselves with it within a single exploration turn. Weapons are typically stored on racks, so that they can be chosen and wielded with a single combat action.

Denser storage is possible for gear that is not intended to be immediately accessible; every 25 square feet of space can hold enough nonmagical gear for 10 medium sized creatures.

**Library -** A properly stocked research library can contain 200 books for every 25 square feet of space. Space may also be dedicated to various other objects, including writing desks, alchemical laboratories, museum displays, an astronomical observatory, or the like. An exceptional workshop may dedicate its supplemental features to a specific Lore or Science task when designing the workshop.

**Larder -** Larders store food and other perishable supplies; every 25 square feet of larder can hold enough food for 5 Medium creatures to eat comfortably for a month.

**Workshops -** Alchemist’s laboratories, blacksmith’s forges, and other crafting workshops can be built to accommodate the needs of any artisans on staff. The size of a workshop depends on the type of craft practiced within it; a jeweler’s workbench only needs 100 square feet, while a woodshop or an iron smithy needs at least 500.

An architect sufficiently versed in Science might design an exceptional workshop with an integrated power source, such as a windmill or a water-wheel.

**Other Facilities -** Additional halls and chambers may be constructed for specific purposes - a gaming hall for gambling and indoor tournaments, or a council room for discussing matters of state.

*Exceptional Facilities -* An architect may

choose to design a workshop, library, infirmary, or stable so that it supports a specific task. For workshops, this is a specific Finesse task; for libraries, this is a specific Lore, Science, or Medicine task; for a stable, this is usually a specific kind of animal. All Great Halls, Chapels, and Infirmaries are automatically considered specialized facilities, since each is dedicated to a specific task by design.

If the architect succeeds at each of a hard Science and Lore check during the design and construction of a specialized facility, and the facility maintains the highest quality of supplies, it is considered to be exceptional – making all task checks easier within the purview of the task it was designed for.

#### Designing The Bailey

After the work crew has finished with the construction of the castle, their services will typically be offered to the villagers of the surrounding bailey, to build their homes and shops and stables. Each such structure is blessed and consecrated by the village priest. Libraries, quarters, stables, and workshops are built as needed, until the entire town settles into its final shape.

Fields are also laid out at this time, with the keep’s lord giving each farmer a parcel to farm, and informing them of the taxes they will be expected to pay come harvest-time.

Druids or priests devoted to the Nature domain will usually bless these fields to increase their yields, and then the farmers will get to work ploughing and sowing. Smaller indoor greenhouses can also be built, to house more delicate plants and fungi that need a controlled environment to thrive.

Each half-mile subhex of sufficiently fertile land can be divided into up to 100 acres, each of which is a single field capable of producing enough crops to feed one or two persons for a year. Most farmland must be ploughed and sowed in spring, and harvested in late summer to early fall; farmers typically rely on the ritual calendars maintained by their priests and druids to sow and reap with the seasons. Adequate larder and silo space must be devoted to crops, to ensure that the harvest is available year-round to feed the domain.

### Populating the Domain

Most domains are established by a particular set of peoples, who desire to live within a specific place and who have chosen their ruler to protect them. These people form the backbone of the new domain’s community, which will hopefully then expand through birth and recruitment.

A knight commissioned to establish a new domain will typically be given vassals to travel with them and help build their keep; those vassals will then stay and become the core of the new population. Further expansion can then be accomplished through recruitment, which means sending heralds out to find suitably skilled commoners and entice them to move into the domain.

#### Laborers

The majority of a domain’s population will be peasant farmers, who provide the backbreaking labor needed to turn arable land into food. Without them, everyone else in the domain would starve.

#### Craftsmen

Craftsmen form the next economic tier above the laborer. Blacksmiths, woodworkers, healers, tailors, and leatherworkers are all needed to keep a domain working, and can all earn a fair living working their trade within the domain.

#### Merchants

The food produced by farmers and the wares produced by craftsmen are bought and sold by merchants, who travel from domain to domain to buy things where they are in abundance and sell them where they are needed.

#### Soldiers-at-Arms

Knights and mercenaries make up the defense and police force of a domain; they represent the will of the nobility among the common people, and so are usually held in grudgingly higher esteem.

#### Clergy and Nobility

At the very top of the hierarchy are the clergy and nobility, who set the pace of the domain’s activities from month to month.

### Unusual Domains

Dwarven and fey domains are typically built to somewhat different standards than human domains. Dwarves prefer to build most of their structures underground, leaving the terrain above for farming and hunting. Fey domains build their structures into the living forest, enchanting trees to grow into the desired shapes of dwellings and civic infrastructure.

Dwarven knowledge did not suffer the setbacks of the Successor Wars to nearly the extent of the Successor Kingdoms, and so most Dwarven settlements can manage to maintain mines and fortresses underground without unduly affecting the fertility of the land above. Any land that is undermined by a dwarven settlement reduces its fertility rating by one step during initial construction, but its fertility is not further reduced by active mining or excavation. A dwarven chapel is also their community’s primary workshop, and is typically placed near to the mines, smelters and refineries — a layout that speaks volumes about the values, beliefs, and practices of dwarven culture.

Fey settlements are typically built around a central sacred grove, with the land around it enhanced through magic until it suits the inhabitants’ desires. This process raises the fertility of the land by one step while the fey tribe dwells within it and actively maintains their fertility rituals. The sacred grove serves as the settlement’s chapel, while the great hall is typically constructed nearby.

Fey settlements will often contain various small gates into and out of Faerie, maintained by druids through repeated use of the misty step ritual. These gateways may not even be noticeable to human or dwarf visitors, leading to a disorienting experience of stepping in and out of Faerie as one wanders from landmark to landmark.

### Domain Administration

A feudal lord has the right to command the subjects living within the protective aegis of their keep, either recruiting them directly into their retinue or issuing proclamations and orders. A lord who is too demanding or inept can expect the attitude of their subjects to worsen as they suffer under their rule; if their general attitude becomes unfriendly or hostile, the lord can expect to be replaced by their sovereign, censured by their own clergy, or might even suffer a tragic accident in short order.

Domains are managed on a month-to-month basis. Once per 30 days, you may issue orders to each of the keep’s facilities, and to the overseers of each vassal living in the bailey. They will then carry out those orders to the best of their abilities, but if orders are consistently nonsensical or burdensome when times are already lean, your vassals’ attitudes are likely to degrade.

Each facility can be issued its own orders:

#### Recruitment

Recruitment orders direct your staff to send heralds to nearby settlements, looking for people with the desired traits that are willing to come and take up residence in the keep or its bailey. Recruitment can be specified in terms of specific talents and abilities. Your heralds roll their Charm and Wit once each to determine whether someone with the correct attributes and willingness is found; the difficulty of each check is based on the rarity of the traits desired, the attitude of your heralds, and your reputation in the areas canvassed.

You can also recruit en masse if you only specify a single, common trait, such as “a good, strong back” or “a sharp mind” or “experience handling a weapon” - if you do, then the lower of each herald’s Charm or Wit roll result determines the number of hirelings recruited by that herald during the month.

#### Crafting

Crafting orders command the artisans in your direct employ to begin producing goods for your use. You may also hire any of the bailey’s craftsmen to assist, negotiating a fair wage for each and supplying them with the necessary facilities and materials. As with domain construction, skilled craftsmen typically expect an amount of gold per month equal to ten times their experience level, plus the cost of any necessary tools and raw materials.

**Magic Items -** A single enchanter with two assistants can attempt to produce a single Common magic item within the month, if given blueprints and all the necessary supplies. A 5 hd or better enchanter with two 3 hd or better assistants can produce 1d6 identical Common items, or attempt to make a single Uncommon item, which will take 1d6÷2 months to complete (round up). A 9 hd enchanter with five 3 hd or better assistants can begin work on a single Rare magic item, which will take 2d6 months to complete.

**Alchemy -** Each alchemist in your employ can be directed to brew stable alchemical potions during the month. At the end of the month, each alchemist rolls Medicine or Science to determine the total number of usable potions that they produced, each producing 1d6-1 on failure or 2d6 + their Intelligence on success. **Arms and Armor -** Any craftsmen trained in blacksmithing may produce weapons and armor for the keep. A blacksmith can produce 5d6 serviceable weapons or shields per month, and a 5 hd or better blacksmith can produce 1d6 masterwork (+1) weapons.

Making armor is somewhat slower; a blacksmith can produce 3d6 serviceable light armors or medium shields, 2d6 serviceable medium armors or shields, or 1d6 serviceable heavy armors. Commissioning masterwork armors from a master blacksmith means 1d6÷2 shields or medium armors per month, or a single heavy armor.

#### Farming and Ranching

Most domains can sustain some amount of farming; sufficiently fertile land can even support itself while providing surplus to store or sell. Different crops and livestock have different land and labor needs, which any experienced peasant will know by heart.

Each half-mile sub-hex of land within a sixmile hex will have its own fertility value, which is a d6 dice formula (such as 3d6) that determines how much food it can yield without being depleted. One “unit” of fertility is equal to one month’s worth of food for one person, produced from one acre of land area.

| Land Quality Fertility Value |  | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| Blighted | 0 | Impossible |
| Barren | 1d6-1 | Hard |
| Marginal | 2d6 | Normal |
| Fertile | 2d6+3 | Normal |
| Abundant | 2d6+8 | Easy |

Each half-mile sub-hex of farmland divides into approximately 200 acres, which can then be apportioned among the domain’s farmers.

Standard farming practice across most of the Successor Kingdoms rotates through land use on a three-year cycle, with each ten-acre field growing grain one year, then legumes the next, and finally being left fallow for the grazing of livestock on the third. A single half-mile sub-hex will typically be divided between six peasant families, each of which manages thirty acres of farmland; the final twenty acres are typically given over to a local druid or nature priest who advises each family, treats any injury and illness, and negotiates with local fair folk when needed. Different kinds of crops and livestock have different harvest seasons, which determine when the land actually produces food or other supplies (such as leather or wool). Harvest is typically conducted as a domain-wide ritual, overseen by priests or druids. The overseer of each sub-hex makes a Survival check come harvest; on a failure, the fertility value of the hex has been reduced by 1 during the year.

Then, roll the sub-hex’s fertility value – this is the total amount of food that the sub-hex produces, measured in person-years of consumption. The lord takes the halftithe; then, each family needs five person-years to survive, with the rest going to market.

#### Hunting and Fishing

Fertile land that is kept wild may be hunted or fished to provide supplemental foodstock and alchemical ingredients. Each month, the warden must decide whether to harvest lightly, normally, or heavily, with a yield of 1d6, 2d6, or 3d6 months of supply per parcel. Then the local warden must make an easy, normal, or hard Survival roll; failure indicates that the land has become depleted and must be left fallow for a time to replenish itself.

#### Mining and Quarrying

Domains that are rich in mineral resources may direct their vassals to mine or quarry them out of the ground, producing a steady revenue stream at the cost of damaging the land’s capacity to sustain wildlife or agriculture. Different kinds of mines or quarries will yield different kinds of resources, from precious metals and gems, to stone and iron for building, to materia and other rare alchemical ingredients. The maximum labor capacity of mineral veins will be determined by the referee when the domain is  established.

Each month, a (2d6+3) roll is made to determine the mine’s yield, multiplied by the number of 50-man (or 25-dwarf) work crews assigned to the mine. As always, the lord is entitled to their halftithe.

| Type of Ore Deposit | Output per 50-man crew |
|---|---|
| Iron | (2d6+3) x 75 lb |
| Stone | (2d6+3) x 75 tons |
| Copper | (2d6+3) x 250 lb |
| Silver | (2d6+3) x 20 lb |
| Gemstones | (2d6+3) x 4 carat |
| Gold | (2d6+3) x 3 lb |

The process of mining and refining riches from the earth is invariably detrimental to the fertility of the land, blighting a mine’s sub-hex and reducing the fertility of each surrounding sub-hex by two steps for each month that the mine is operational. Sufficient irrigation and runoff control force the problem downstream, automatically blighting the downstream subhex and reducing the fertility of each further downstream sub-hex by one step for 2d6 miles. Druids, numina and fair folk will tend to find this process horrifically disgraceful, and are likely to mount armed retaliation if the runoff begins to affect their sacred groves.

#### Merchant Activities

Crafted goods, food produced by farms, game captured through hunting and fishing, and riches produced from mining and quarrying can all be used or stored up to a point, but selling them at market (and purchasing any wares or resources that the domain is experiencing a shortfall in) is often much more lucrative. Merchants purchase goods at one price and sell at another; usually, this involves traveling from domain to domain and purchasing goods where they are in plentiful supply, to sell later where they are in higher demand.

As such, it is often difficult for the lord of a domain to determine exactly how to apply the halftithe to a merchant’s profits. The usual custom is to simply demand a small tax at the point of sale, recorded and tallied and paid monthly from the merchant to the lord who graciously hosted their shop or stall; merchants who travel on a quicker schedule will need to square their books and pay their taxes each time they enter or leave a domain. To ease calculations, most taxes are collected from the seller at a rate of 10% of the gross earnings of each sale, less purchases made while within the domain; if proper records cannot be produced, then 10% of the merchant’s total cash reserves are confiscated instead.

#### Minting Coin

Gold, silver, and copper mined from the domain can be minted into currency. In most of the Successor Kingdoms, strict laws prevent anyone below a baron from commissioning the minting of copper coins, anyone below a duke from commissioning the minting of silver, and anyone below a prince or king from commissioning the minting of gold. Thus raw gold, silver, and copper ingots filter their way up through the halftithe, are minted into coins at the appropriate level of the feudal hierarchy, and then enter circulation where they filter back down to the populace.

In a mint, each pound of copper or silver ingot is pressed into 16 coins, and each pound of gold ingot is pressed into 32 gold coins, after assaying and alloying to the proper ratios of metal.

#### Research

Research orders assign a team of qualified scholars and craftsmen to discover historical information, or to help design a new arcane spell, magic item blueprint, or alchemical formula.

Player characters can spend a good portion of their downtime researching ancient lore and arcane secrets, and parties with a suitably furnished domain can direct their vassals to perform research on their behalf. The first step for any research project is to establish the core domain of the research, which determines the talent used to gather the raw data:

**Science -** designing a new arcane spell or ritual; designing a new alchemical potion based on an arcane spell effect that isn’t a poison or elixir; determining the cause and nature of an unknown supernatural event; designing a new magic item that duplicates a known spell effect or that implements a known scientific principle; developing a new architectural or metallurgic technique; expanding the frontiers of scientific knowledge..

**Medicine -** diagnosing a rare disease, discovering the vectors that spread it, and developing a treatment and prevention plan; designing a new alchemical elixir or poison based on an understood arcane spell effect; using already-understood arcane and fey energies to produce a new species of fey beast; working out a nutrition plan for a city under siege whose supplies are dwindling.

**Lore -** Uncovering the lineage of a foundling prince; finding the original cause of a generational feud between two houses, finding an obscure clause or loophole in a centuries-old contract with the local Numina; translating an ancient text written in a longdead language; inventing a new art style.

**Survival -** determining why the local peasants’ crops are wilting; understanding the impact of introducing or eradicating a species from the local ecosystem; breeding a new plant species through hybridization.

<!-- p. 272 -->
Once the domain of research is determined, available research materials are gathered.

Useful tomes, handwritten notes, and eyewitness anecdotes all add to the volume of information that must be sorted through, categorized, and filtered for accuracy and relevance. The total amount of information gathered, and the obscurity of the relevant facts within that information, set the base difficulty of the task.

Most research is theoretical, only requiring that the research team pours through their collected information and come to the right conclusion. Certain forms of research, however, require active experimentation, often exposing the research team to genuine danger. The referee will determine whether a particular research project requires experimentation or not, but Science and Medicine projects almost always require research, while Lore and Survival projects almost never do.

At the end of each week of research, each member of the research team rolls their talent vs. the difficulty determined by the referee, to determine whether that researcher manages to make useful sense of the relevant information. Each researcher that succeeds then rolls 1d6 + their Intelligence modifier, adding to an accumulating pool of research points; a particularly disastrous failure instead indicates a magical mishap or wildly inaccurate deduction that sets back the whole project, subtracting that researcher’s research point roll from the accumulated research point pool.

The referee will determine how many research points are necessary before success is reached. Success means the creation of a new work, summarizing the research team’s findings - for a Science or Medicine research project, that new work might be a spell, blueprint, or alchemical formula ready to be copied into a spellbook.

### Gaining Experience

The professionals and experts of a village will typically gain experience as they perform their duties, albeit at a much slower rate than adventurers. Each year that a professional with class levels spends managing an aspect of your domain, they gain one experience. If a particularly arduous emergency occurs during a particular year, which they personally attended to, that professional might earn two experience during that year.

Professional NPCs gain class levels at the same experience rate as player characters. If brought along on an adventure, they gain experience at the rate of one per adventure, just like a player character.

### Defense and Siege

During a siege, all other activity ceases, as the occupants of the bailey retreat to the motte and guards man the towers. Any professional NPCs with combat-appropriate class levels, such as priests, druids, scouts, or warriors, can be directed to aid in the defense of the domain as needed. Peasants, merchants, and other denizens without class levels will either be placed into logistic roles, or mustered into pike formations and placed under the supervision of a trained warrior.

Most keeps will construct various defenses to use in the event of a siege, from battlements and defense towers to siege weapons like ballistae or cannons. Design and construction of these defenses is handled using the artifact construction rules.

## Arcane Spells

Arcane magic is dedicated to producing reliable results based on understood aetheric principles. A wizard spends most of their time studying these abstract principles, with actual spellcasting seen as simply a rote application of those principles.

### Arcana, Materia, and Soma

Each castable arcane spell has three components, called the spell’s Arcana, Materia, and Soma.

A spell’s **Arcana** is the specific configuration of runes and sigils as laid out in the wizard’s spellbook. Each arcane spell has a unique Arcana, which can be copied from one spellbook to another.

A spell’s **Materia** is the magical “fuel” used to help power the spell; this is usually a material component with magical properties, which is ground into powder during the spell’s preparation to create magical ink, then painted onto the wizard’s body in a pattern following the runes and sigils dictated by the spell’s Arcana. This pattern is nearly complete, only requiring a trigger to perfectly match the Arcana described in your spellbook.

A spell’s **Soma** is the specific triggering words or gestures that must be performed by the wizard to “complete” the spell, activating and consuming the prepared Materia and producing the spell’s effect. Each spell’s Arcana must be placed in an appropriate place on the wizard’s body, and the wizard’s skill and training determine how large and precise the spell’s Arcana must be in order to be reliably triggered by the spell’s Soma.

Most spells have both a verbal soma and a kinetic soma – that is, they require both speech and precise motion to activate. Trying to cast while deafened or restrained requires a hard Science check to successfully execute the spell; Attempting to cast while silenced simply fails altogether, unless the spell requires no verbal somatic component. On a failure, the spell is wasted in a flash of light with no other effect.

### Preparation

Within the two-hour span around sunrise or sunset, you may spend time preparing arcane spells from your spellbook. Spell preparation is easiest when the veil between worlds is thinnest, in the hour before or after dawn or before or after sunset; a wizard may also prepare their chosen school’s spells outside of this window, but with more difficulty. Each prepared spell lasts until you cast it, or until after the next sunrise or sunset.

Each spell to be prepared requires ⅙ of a bottle of magic ink per circle. This ink is consumed during the spell preparation process as it is applied directly to the wizard's skin; the ink must contain ground-up and emulsified Materia with the same aspect as the spell. Most spells have a trigger-sigil prepared onto one of the wizard’s hands, which must be free and empty in order to cast the spell. The remainder is prepared onto other portions of the wizard’s body, which cannot be covered by armor in order for the spell’s Arcana to properly function – wearing any armor other than cloth halves the total number of spells that a wizard may prepare.

### Spell Scrolls

You may choose to use specially prepared parchment, rod, and inks while preparing a spell to create a spell scroll instead of preparing it onto your body; if you spend one full day from sunrise to sunset (or sunset to sunrise) enchanting a single scroll, that scroll becomes supernaturally resilient, and will retain its potency indefinitely until used.

A scroll prepared by a wizard may be used by any other wizard of an appropriate level to cast the prepared spell. Non-wizards, or wizards who are not of a sufficient level to cast spells of that circle, may also attempt to cast a spell from a scroll by first spending an action to save vs. Trick, with a difficulty based on the scroll’s circle. If they succeed, they cast the spell as their next turn’s action.

A wizard may also attempt to reverseengineer a spell from a scroll and record the complete spell in their spellbook; this takes an hour of research and destroys the scroll. The wizard saves vs. confusion with a difficulty based on the scroll’s circle; if they succeed, they add the spell to their spellbook.

### Spell Schools

Each arcane spell always belongs to two schools of magic: one set of schools is called its Substance, and the other is called its Form. Each arcane spell always has one Substance and one Form. There are five Forms and three Substances, although wizards argue incessantly about whether there are really three Substances, seven, or twelve.

#### Arcane Forms

The four Forms are Abjuration, Conjuration, Divination, and Transmutation. An Arcane Form shapes the “intent” of a spell towards a specific goal: abjurations protect from harm or change, conjurations manifest raw magical substances or energies and weave them into specific forms, divinations reveal information to the caster or provide a creature with new sensory abilities, and transmutations change one substance or shape into another.

#### Arcane Substances

While the Form of a spell determines its intent, the Substance of the spell defines the energies that will be commanded to accomplish that intent. There are three Form schools - aetheric, psychic, and elemental - with the elemental school further divided into four elements of Earth, Water, Air, and Flame.

#### Aetheric

*spells affect the raw tapestry of*  

material reality itself, twisting and re-weaving the fundamental relationships between events that mortals call time, space, motion, and probability. Aetheric spells that cause harm inflict aetheric damage.

#### Psychic

*spells affect the thoughts, beliefs,*  

actions, perceptions, and emotions of sentient beings. Harmful psychic spells inflict psychic damage.

#### Elemental

*spells depend on their prepared*  

materia to determine their damage type.

Wizards recognize four types of elemental materia (earth, water, air, and flame), each with two corresponding damage types based on polarity (yin and yang).

*Stone* (Yin earth) deals petrification damage

by transforming flesh and wood into sandstone, metal into rust, and liquids into mud. A creature being petrified can save vs. Hold to arrest the progress of the affliction.

*Metal* (Yang earth) is affected by armor like

normal weapon damage, and can deal piercing, blunt, or sundering damage depending on the spell.

*Ice* (Yin water) deals cold damage, slowing

and freezing the target. Rolling a '6' for cold damage forces the victim to save vs. Hold or be hobbled or immobilized by the ice.

*Acid* (Yang water) deals acid damage, which

sunders armor and weapons on a damage roll of '6'. A wizard may choose to cast any acid spell non-lethally by spraying the target with seawater rather than acid, dealing blunt damage instead.

*Lightning* (Yin air) deals lightning damage,

which stuns affected creatures on a damage roll of '6' unless they save vs. Hold. All saving throws vs. lightning-based spells are harder if a creature is wearing metal armor or wielding a medium or heavy metal weapon.

*Thunder* (Yang air) deals thunder damage,

which deafens affected creatures on a damage roll of '6' unless they save vs. Blast.

*Radiant* (Yin fire) deals radiant damage, flash-

burning the target with intense light and blinding them on a damage roll of '6' unless they save vs. Blast.

*Flame* (Yang fire) deals fire damage, roasting

the target with intense heat and igniting their flesh and possessions on a damage roll of '6' unless they save vs. Blast.

### Creating A New Arcane Spell

When not adventuring, wizards spend most of their time performing arcane research.

Much of this research is theoretical, but sometimes a wizard makes a discovery that provides practical results.

Every Arcane spell known was created by a Wizard at some point in the distant past.

Player characters may wish to create their own spells; doing so should follow the following guidelines:

#### Research Points

Each month that a wizard spends in their laboratory researching a spell design, they make a Science test with a difficulty determined by the desired circle of the spell; if they succeed, they accumulate 1d6 research points.

If the wizard has a magic item or item blueprint, or a similar magical effect to work backwards from that they can reliably experiment upon, their Science test is easy.

#### Designing the Spell

Once a wizard has accumulated enough research points to attempt to design their spell, they may do so. Actually attempting the spell design involves a final Science test to avoid a mishap. If this Science test fails, the referee will choose some kind of spell effect appropriate to the spell being designed, which manifests during the attempt to design the spell with chaotic results. Otherwise, the new spell works as intended, and is added to the wizard’s spellbook.

Non-damaging spells require significantly more referee judgment than spells designed to simply deal hit point damage. The best guideline is to begin with an existing spell, and then adjust its parameters until the players and referee all agree on the final spell’s balance and required research.

#### Determining Spell Range

Spells that affect the caster themselves, or only creatures that they touch, do not need to devote any research points to range. Area effect spells that originate from the caster's space also do not need to devote research points to range. Otherwise, each 5 paces of range costs 1 research point, up to a maximum range of 20 paces for a first-circle spell, 50 paces for a second-circle spell, or 100 paces for a third-circle spell.

Spells with unusual effects may have their maximum possible range limited by the referee more stringently.

**Area of effect** is similarly limited by research points - a spell can affect 6 square paces of area per research point, and the total amount of research that can be put into range, area, and damage together cannot exceed 6 research points per circle. The approximate ‘square pace area’ of various radii, cones, and lines as follows:

#### Determining Spell Damage

Spells that instantaneously inflict damage are relatively straightforward. Most spells that directly inflict damage are conjurations, although some transmutations can also instantaneously damage their targets.

The maximum theoretical damage that a spell can do is 3d6 per circle for a melee attack or close-range area, or 2d6 per circle for a ranged attack or long-range area; each research point put into damage translates into 1d6 damage.

Spells that harm or hinder the target without inflicting dice of damage need to be balanced individually on a case-by-case basis.

A damaging spell can be made to cause one elemental effect per damage die that rolls ‘6’; this costs 1 research point per 2 dice of damage, and the maximum number of research points that can be spent this way is equal to the spell’s circle. A spell can also automatically inflict an elemental effect on a failed saving throw; each elemental effect that is automatically inflicted costs 2 research points, and reduces the spell’s maximum damage by 1 die.

Some damaging transmutations can operate more slowly than conjurations, gradually transforming a creature or object into a less functional form. The maximum damage per round of a sustained damaging transmutation is limited to one die per spell circle. Each die of direct ongoing damage costs 2 research points, and automatically inflicts an elemental effect on a '6'; each elemental effect directly inflicted regardless of damage costs 3 research points.

#### Non-Damaging Spells

Non-damaging spells will require direct negotiation with the referee to determine the spell’s circle and limitations.

**Abjurations** require slightly more referee judgment than damaging transmutations or conjurations. Each die of temporary hit points granted to one creature is approximately the equivalent of one die of damage dealt, and so costs 1 research point. Shields that erect barriers which protect everyone in an area should have a similar cost as conjurations that damage everyone within that area, so the area of effect research cost is also the same. Making saving throws easier or harder is approximately equivalent to granting critical hit chances, and so the costs there can be directly translated as well. Beyond this, some amount of tweaking will be required to line up the spell’s research point cost with established spells.

Finally, **divinations** and non-damaging utility spells of any school must be adjudicated on a case-by-case basis by the referee, using existing spells as an example to judge the new spell's circle, effects, and requirements.

#### Spell Design Examples

Fireball represents nearly the theoretical maximum power for conjuration spells that inflict damage. Fireball inflicts six dice of elemental damage (+6 research points) in a 20’ blast radius (+8 research points) within 20 paces (+4 research points), and ignites one target for each die that rolls a ‘6’ (+3 research points), for a total of 21 research points.

Lightning bolt also inflicts six dice of elemental damage (+6 research points), doing so in a 3-pace wide line 30 paces long (+10 research points), and inflicts a stun effect on each die that rolls a ‘6’ (+3 research points), for a total of 19 research points.

## So You Really Wanna Do This

Being a referee for a D20 fantasy game can be an incredibly fun and rewarding experience. It’s also a *lot* of work. This book is mostly a collection of guidelines for managing and constructing adventures; as referee, you are free to modify them as you see fit. Keep in mind that more experienced players might be surprised if you change a long-standing rule.

## Setting the Theme

Before you get started, it’s good to understand the kind of adventures you’re going to want to referee for. Theme is a tricky topic to tackle, because it encompasses so many facets of the story. Do you want a light-hearted adventure? Comedy? Romance? Horror? These thematic elements set the ‘flavor’ of an adventure, and an adventure can shift from one theme to another across the arc of a campaign. One evening might call for cartoon-like hĳinks, the next for a nerve-wracking confrontation with terrors from beyond space and time.

A good media library is an excellent place to gather inspiration for the themes of your campaign. Most roleplaying systems will provide you with a list of ‘suggested fiction’ that capture the moods and aesthetics of their game world; often this list is called “Appendix N” in deference to Gygax and Arneson’s list, placed in the back of the first edition of the forefather of modern fantasy gaming. Our own “Appendix N” is in the back of this book; we heartily recommend sitting down with some of the media listed there if looking for inspiration.

**The Talk -** It’s generally a good idea to sit down with your players and have the talk.

“The talk” is a frank discussion of boundaries, concerns, and topics that the players would rather not have to deal with in the campaign. Some players might be uncomfortable with sexual themes; others might have issues with domestic violence, or slavery, or any number of topics. Talking this over before the group begins helps give a healthy orientation towards the goals of the game.

## Setting the Pace

One of the first things you should decide, as a group, is the real-world pace of your campaign. This will involve juggling schedules until the referee and all the players can agree on a consistent time and place to sit down and play together. The game was written with the idea that the typical pace for an adventure would be a single four to six hour adventure per week, typically on a weekend. This pacing gives your referee enough time to prepare, and the players time to unwind and socialize after the game. At this pace, a set of first-level adventurers can be expected to reach 10th level at the end of one year of play, assuming that nobody dies. (Someone is definitely going to die.) A solid year for a campaign to go from first-level novices to tenth-level heroes is a good run. You can accelerate this process by rewarding players who contribute positively to the gaming experience with additional milestones more frequently. A player group that is consistently vibrant, in good spirits, and engaged with the setting is a joy to behold, and actions that support that experience should be rewarded. Don’t be tempted into rewarding “good roleplaying” when that roleplaying involves stabbing the group in the back, unless everyone at the table agrees that the sudden but inevitable betrayal actually contributed positively to the group’s enjoyment of the game. (It usually doesn’t, but there are always exceptions.)

If a player goes out of their way to help a less experienced player out, you should consider rewarding both of them. It takes a lot of courage to sit down at a new table and roll up a level one character alongside a party of seasoned sixth-level adventurers; giving the new kid an extra milestone here and there will ensure they reach parity with the rest of the party sooner rather than later. That said, a first-level adventurer still has plenty to contribute to a sixth-level party, so make sure that everyone is having fun regardless of their difference in power levels.

## Setting the Scope

A good low-level adventure (levels 1 to 3)

should feel dangerous and exciting, but also personal. The player characters are just setting out in the world, and learning to make their mark in it; they should usually feel like a small part in a much bigger world. Of course, their actions and consequences won’t stay small for long.

Most player characters at level 1 will be young and inexperienced, typically between 13 and 21 years of age. An older first-level player character should have a particularly good reason why they are just now setting out adventuring, and with so little life experience and competence to show for it.

Many referees start their party’s first three levels in some kind of “training” scenario – either in a school environment or under the tutelage of older, more experienced adventurers – to help manage expectations and guide new players safely through their first adventures. By level 3, the party is just barely competent enough to manage real lowlevel threats on an even footing; making level three the “transition into the real world” can give new players a valuable sense of accomplishment.

The “magical training academy” trope is a popular young adult fiction genre for a reason; if you choose to start your adventurers at level 1 in some kind of fantasy classroom environment, make sure to choose themes with an appropriate maturity level so that the players’ expectations don’t feel violated.

Another theme for an early-game story is a group of young adventurers forced out of their homes by a disaster, forcing them to venture out into the world and develop competence quickly.

By mid-story (levels 3 to 4), the adventuring party should feel established in their local community, known for solving problems that others can’t; by this level, the community and local nobility should be coming to them for help whenever faced with threats too large for the local peacekeepers. All this depends on the relationships and reputations that the players have fostered for their characters, of course; a band of marauding ne’er-do-wells will have to secure their own opportunities. High-level adventures (levels 4 and 5) tend to affect entire kingdoms; characters with good reputations and relationships with the local nobility have likely gained titles, land, and renown of their own by now, and are firmly entrenched into the politics of their kingdom. Play doesn’t have to stop at level 5, of course. High-end campaigns can be a lot of fun, although the kinds of adventures that highlevel characters get up to will be significantly different than those they struggled with as beginning adventurers. High-level play typically has consequences that extend far beyond the player characters themselves, regularly affecting not just their own kingdoms, but the shape of the whole world.

In fact, adventures beyond level 5 could easily become the primary scope of your campaign, with the early adventures simply existing to provide backstory to describe the party’s humble beginnings. If you want to engage in this kind of ‘epic’-style play, it’s probably good to have at least one short “prelude”

adventure at each class level before beginning the “real campaign” at level 5.

## Setting the Rules

Once everyone has agreed on themes, pace, and scope, it’s time to work out the rules that everyone will be expected to play by. The Player’s Guide presents the ‘core rules’ of the game, with Setting Guides sometimes providing optional rules that you and your players might choose to incorporate.

Appendix M of this book also gives several examples of alternate rules that the table might want to try.

However, the “rules of the game” are not the only rules that need to be discussed. You should also discuss what kinds of behavior will be encouraged, discouraged, or simply not allowed at your table; etiquette and mutual respect are far more important for a good gaming experience than a good rulebook. As the referee, it’s your duty to keep the game flowing smoothly and to deal with any trouble that occurs, and it’s the players’ collective duty to abide by whatever rules you lay down at your table.

## There and Back Again

## Setting over Story

In a “dungeon crawl”, the party as a whole creates a collaborative story of their adventures. As the referee, your job is to provide them with the setting where this story takes place. The players will then create the story of the adventure based on their choices and interactions with that environment.

This means that you should never expect events to play out in a particular way, if your players have anything at all to say about it. And your players should always have some say in the unfolding of events - otherwise they’re spectators, not players.

A good setting still has a lot going on in the background, however. The party should enter the story with the sense of a world already set into motion; various NPCs and forces have their own agendas and talents, and may already be well on their way to completing their plans - but the players’ actions have immediate and profound impact on those machinations, forcing NPCs to react to their presence. This helps weave the impression of a living world, where interesting things happen and the players’ choices matter.

The setting you provide your players should have a history and a reason for being the way it is. This history will contain elements that a character might recall or discover during research with a good Lore check, or that a character with sufficient Charm and Wit might discover by chatting up the locals.

Backstory and history are also how most player character parties get hooked into adventures in the first place – a party could spend years wandering the wilderness and hoping to stumble onto something worthwhile, but if the local village elders tell tales to their children about a hidden mine full of treasure deep in the mountains to the East, the party has a place to start looking.

## Rulings over Rules

It can be tempting, as a referee, to create specific, detailed rules for various things the players might want to attempt. The game works much more smoothly, however, if you focus on the players’ actions and choices to decide whether a particular action succeeds or fails, falling back on saving throws and task checks (and quickly ruling on appropriate difficulty levels) only when there’s some degree of luck involved.

### Deciding Difficulty

As a referee, your job is to simulate the game world. The players’ collective job is to decide how to interact with that game world, to reach the outcomes they desire. They describe their actions, and you determine the consequences of those actions. In determining those consequences, your first tools are common sense and intuition about how the game world works. Your second tools, after common sense and intuition, are the dice and rules.

What this means in practice is that if the players describe an action that will obviously work, and that is obviously doable, just let them do it and move on. There’s no sense in rolling the dice at all. And on the flipside, if they describe an action that simply isn’t logically possible given how the world works, even if the rules imply that they can do it, you have every right to say “no, those rules don’t apply here” and declare that they can’t achieve that outcome with that action.

Remember that you need to be fair, consistent, and reasonable with this power, or you will quickly find yourself without very many players who want to play in your games.

This principle is often referred to as “rulings, not rules”. It is said to be one of the cornerstones of “old-school play”, whatever those words mean.

Regardless, once you’ve handled all situations automatic or impossible, what remains is those situations with some probable chance of success. These are the situations that attack throws, saving throws, and task checks are for, and the rules divide them into easy, normal, and hard tasks.

### Easier and Harder

Various class feats, equipment, magics, and specific circumstances are explicitly described in the rules as making a particular set of rolls easier or harder. Beyond that, anything that you think should intuitively make an action noticeably easier or harder for the character should do so. Multiple instances of “easier” and “harder” cancel each other out, leaving one or more “easier” or “harder” modifiers stacked onto each other.

### Automatic or Impossible

If it seems like the total number of “easier” or “harder” modifiers are overwhelming, use your intuition to decide whether they should combine to make the roll automatic or impossible. As a rule of thumb, saving throws and attack throws should almost never become automatic or impossible; task checks should never become impossible for experts, and should never become automatic for anyone else. A caveat is for actions that are obviously not physically possible under any circumstances, such as a scout attempting to "climb" a volley of crossbow bolts fired at them; as the referee, it's your job to decide whether you want to set the boundaries of possibility based on the real world, the cinematic internal logic of action movies, or the aesthetics of wuxia cinema. If an expert wants to try something that simply doesn't fit the theme of your campaign, feel free to explain that there's impossible and there's impossible, and work with them to come up with a different idea or approach.

Besides external circumstances, another source of difficulty modifiers that referees should track is the collection of innate advantages and disadvantages granted by a character’s origin. Small creatures in particular, or creatures with unusual anatomy, will often have an easier or harder time in a wide variety of circumstances compared to an ‘average’ human player character. As the referee, it is your job to think about the environmental circumstances and the character’s anatomy, and from there determine what is likely to be easy or hard. Don’t go overboard with this, though; small characters or creatures with unusual anatomy are used to their body shapes, and will have spent their whole lives learning how to handle unusual circumstances in their own ways.

## Random Encounters, Random Loot

One way to help your own mindset is to use random tables to determine what kinds of dangers and rewards the players encounter, rather than hard-and-fast lists of monsters and treasure. When you roll on a random list, you don’t know any more than the players do what they’re about to run into - which means that you are forced to improvise.

This doesn’t mean abandoning the setting, however! As you roll on random tables, try to come up with plausible explanations for why this particular monster is in this particular room with that particular treasure, and then add small descriptive flourishes to hint at those explanations. As your sense of what’s going on “behind the scenes” unfolds, feel free to adjust the results of various tables accordingly, even ignoring and re-rolling certain results, to keep the unfolding narrative consistent – but always allow the dice to have their say, too. Being a referee means learning to paint with a palette handed to you by a fickle universe, but it can be an incredibly rewarding skill to hone.

Having random tables also doesn’t mean that you can’t have named NPCs with detailed plans and schemes – it just means that those NPC’s plans and schemes are just as subject to the whims of fate as your player characters’. Your job as referee is to keep things fair, after all; if the evil sorceress who controls the hordes of undead fails at an important saving throw, she’d better have a good back-up plan! (She almost certainly does; after all, she didn’t get to where she is by being a complete slouch at scheming and planning).

You can use the generic tables given in Appendix E, or create your own. Loot and monster tables should represent a coherent theme, but provide sufficient variation within that theme to represent the fickle finger of fate - an encounter with goblin orcs might mean running into a small scouting party while the main force is off looting a village, or the player characters might blunder into the main force unawares. The dice help you by making many of these decisions for you, letting you weave the story together with the pieces they hand you.

### Pre-Made Descriptions

Many adventures and maps will have descriptions ready for you to read to your players as they enter areas; don’t be afraid to adjust these to suit your own style, but remember to describe important features with enough detail to invite interaction! Also keep in mind that your descriptions shouldn’t give away the consequences of interaction, unless those consequences are obvious at first glance – it’s the players’ jobs to keep their characters out of trouble, not yours.

### Managing Alertness Checks

Alertness checks as the party enters new areas will give you an idea of how much or how little detail to provide. One good habit is to write out what sorts of things are automatically visible to anyone at first sight, what sorts of things require an Alertness check to spot, and what sorts of things are impossible to notice without further detailed inspection of a specific point of interest in the area.

If there are multiple things in the room that could each be spotted with an Alertness check, but each of them is at a different difficulty, you can save time by making a single 2d6 Alertness check for each character, and include everything that would be easy to spot if only one of that character’s dice succeeds, while including everything that is hard to spot if both of their dice succeed.

### Traps and Ambushes

If entering a new area immediately triggers a trap or an ambush, you should describe the situation with more urgency and at a faster pace, to help convey the danger the party finds themselves in. Only focus on the descriptions that the party would automatically notice in these circumstances; anyone who wants to look around and roll Alertness to try to spot subtler details is welcome to spend their combat action doing so.

## Describing Ambiance

Describing the ambiance of a dungeon or wilderness area helps set the “tone” of the adventure, and orients the players to their surroundings. You should consider the “ambient” features of a dungeon at least as important as the game mechanics.

### Sights, Sounds, and Smells

Your players need to use their imagination to put themselves within the game world. A good referee will describe scenes vividly enough that they provide hooks for the players to follow – an odd smell or sound might be the clue that a particular player needs to ask further questions.

Don’t spoon-feed your players with only the information they need to “guess the right password”; a good description should include several different features available to the various senses, with only a few of them sufficiently relevant to warrant deeper investigation.

### Sense of Wonder

This is a world full of ancient historical wonders and eldritch magics; the world beyond the party’s front door should seem vast, strange, and full of surreal beauty. It might take some time to find out what specific aspects of the setting capture your players’ imaginations, but learning to keep them engaged is what makes the difference between an average session and a great one.

Scale is a good tool for producing a sense of wonder. Expansive vistas, impossibly tall cliffs, wide open seas and gently undulating plains all give a sense of a wide-open world, free for the party to go wherever they wish. On the other side, tiny details help reinforce that every square inch of this vast world is filled with more wonders than a lifetime could take in.

### Sense of Dread

An adventuring party is, by definition, a group of thrillseekers getting in way over their heads, in situations that most denizens of the world are wise enough to avoid altogether. The party’s sense of wonder should always be mixed with an appropriate amount of fear and dread, especially in uncanny situations where the supernatural holds sway.

Dread and terror produce a far more satisfying experience when their source is the uncanny, rather than the simply dangerous. A massive beast with impressive combat abilities might be frightening, but subtle hints that things are not quite right with a creature or environment can sustain a much more palpable tension for far longer.

Starting with small details of ambiance also gives you an opportunity to slowly build dread over the course of an adventure. The strange details might start off few and far between, and barely worth noticing, but get more blatant and more frequent as the party approaches the source of the disturbance.

Adventures that eventually wind up passing through Faerie or Shadow often start out this way - tiny disturbances in the environment that subtly hint at an incursion, getting more prevalent and more extreme as one approaches the tear in the veil.

### Sense of Hope

At level 1, the player characters start off fragile and vulnerable. A first-level adventurer is hopelessly outmatched by the dangers of the world just outside the castle walls. And yet, civilization is recovering. The player characters – or people just like them – should feel necessary to the world’s recovery and improvement, and it should feel just barely possible that they might pull it off.

### Wilderness Ambiance

#### The Open Road

Most roads in the Successor Kingdoms were built by the old empire, and are in various states of disrepair. Rich and prosperous kingdoms will have the resources to keep their main roads fairly well-maintained and well-patrolled, while desperate backwaters are more likely to let their roads and watchtowers fall into disrepair or even ruin. Small villages, farms, and pastures are typically constructed along roads, so the countryside will be dotted with periodic signs of civilization. These will typically show the same economic conditions as the roads themselves; if they don’t, that fact itself says something important about the priorities and taxation policies of the area being passed through.

#### Forests

Traveling through forests will slow the party down unless there is an obvious path or an experienced guide to help them navigate.

Even so, the distance that can be seen ahead is severely limited in the forest, making random encounters far more likely.

The deeper one goes into the forest, the more likely it is that the veil will thin and drop the party into Faerie or Shadow. Even in the Prime Material, a deep virgin forest is full of sufficient predators and hazards to challenge even a group of seasoned adventurers.

#### Mountains

Mountains slow the party down as well, without obstructing as much of the view. In fact, depending on the terrain, a trek through the mountains might offer a breathtaking vista for miles around – while line of sight is limited to about a league on flat terrain, being at elevation can extend that to up to six leagues in any unobstructed direction.

Natural hazards in the mountains are much more common than in other terrain types – avalanches, crumbling paths, and steep cliffs can all send the party plummeting to their doom with little warning.

#### Caverns

Traveling underground, of course, combines all the dangers of mountaineering with the limited visibility of a forest journey. Many dwarven villages, mines, and forges still exist underground, some of them still populated and maintained by civilized dwarves. Beyond their reach, however, the caverns stretch deep enough that the veil ceases to hold sway, and Faerie, Shadow, and the Material blend together into a strange place called the Dark. Within the Dark, the energies of Faerie and Shadow blend together to shape the abundant matter of the Material into eerie and terrifying shapes and creatures. Even denizens from the deep Infernal Abyss can sometimes find their way to the Dark and survive, their “biology” far less encumbered by Material physics than they would be above ground.

Even within the safer caverns of the Prime Material, natural hazards are more common than even in the mountains, exacerbated by the utter darkness and claustrophobically low ceilings.

Most of the Dark is uninhabited, but below the Hansa lies a vast subterranean civilization founded by an Orcish coalition of dwarves, elves, and ettercap centaurs. Their civilization is called Svartalfheim by the Hansa, which occasionally trades with it. despite its brutal Infernal ways. Svartalfen grottoes are eerie but beautiful cathedrals of hewn crystal and semiprecious stone, displaying a most elegant merger of elven and dwarven architectural prowess and aesthetic.

#### Wetlands, Rivers and Lakes

Wetlands literally bog the party down in thick mud, while exposing them to biting insects and generally unsanitary conditions. The flora and fauna of a wetland seem to compensate by offering unparalleled beauty and diversity, with each square inch of terrain containing some kind of exquisite hidden gem of flora or fauna.

Crossing a river can be its own miniadventure, as anyone who has played Oregon Trail can attest. The difficulty of fording a river depends on the depth, strength and speed of the current, and whether the party is attempting to cross on foot, mounted, or with a wagon.

Wetlands tend to be some of the most fertile soils available, and rivers and lakes are necessary sources of water for villages, farms, and pastures, so most farmlands will typically be settled nearby, and villages and roads will crop up shortly thereafter. Once a village is sufficiently well-established, the Church will send a priest to consecrate the land, sealing up any inconvenient breaches into Faerie or Shadow that might otherwise be attracted to the moor’s ambiance.

The flowing water of a river can also be harnessed to run mills, smithies, and other forms of light industrialization, saving labor and making the lives of nearby villagers more convenient. And, of course, sufficiently wide and deep rivers offer convenient trade routes for the transportation of goods and persons.

#### The Open Seas

Above the water, the open seas are an unbroken expanse of wave and sky, differentiated only by the day’s weather or the occasional sea bird. Below the waves, the pelagic depths host all forms of strange creatures among the rock and coral.

Sailing the open ocean is its own kind of adventure, where the ship and her crew become the primary setting, and the ocean itself becomes a mere vehicle to deliver wonders and hazards along the voyage.

Small islands and atolls dot the ocean, of course, and larger ones might cover enough hexes to warrant their own terrain types, but anything smaller than a league across will likely be a small patch of sandbar and vegetation.

As with underground caverns, the vastness of the ocean and the lack of anchoring civilizations thins the veil. Open ocean can shift without warning into Fey or Dark waters, especially in violent weather. Sailors may be prone to tall tales, but they are equally likely to have seen inexplicable marvels and horrors as a fey gale or dark storm tore the veil asunder, pulling their ship partially into the Aether.

#### Faerie and Shadow

Faerie, Shadow, and even the Dark typically resemble the wilderness terrain that the party just came from - at least at first. As the party advances, the terrain should become more surreal and more ominous, but always with a veneer of plausible deniability – right until it’s too late to go back.

It can be difficult to distinguish between a “hazard” and a “trap” in Faerie or Shadow – indeed, it can be hard to distinguish between “terrain” and “denizen”. Most of the deeper Realms are shaped by the wills of their inhabitants, such that the terrain can almost be seen as an extension of their imaginations and moods – even creating phantasmal hosts to populate an otherwise barren demesne.

Make sure that each slice of Faerie or Shadow has its own personality, whims, and desires.

### Dungeon Architectural Styles

Ruins should typically maintain a single architectural style throughout, unless the structure has been adapted and expanded by multiple different cultures over the centuries. Various cultures throughout history have built structures which your player character party might explore as dungeons; the architectural style of a dungeon will give clues about what to expect inside.

#### Empires of Antiquity

The archetypical fallen empires from our own history are Rome, Greece, Egypt, and Persia. Imperial ruins might tend towards round stone columns holding up square roofs, realistic frescoes of humanoid heroes and gods, and even, regular spacings of 5’ paces. Waterworks such as fountains, pools, and aqueducts adorn most public spaces, and many floors are decorated with beautiful tile mosaics of high craftsmanship.

#### Ancient Hypertechnology

The ruins of ancient Atlantis, or even ancient alien precursor civilizations, should be vast technological marvels that convey a sense of immense scale and power. All such ruins should express strong “science fantasy”

ideals, perhaps leaning towards the “crystal spires and togas” style of early 20th century Utopian science fiction.

Even the nature of the damage sustained by these ruins should convey a sense of vastness and dread - giant craters, half-melted buildings, and shadows flash-frozen onto walls by energies released from weapons of unimaginable power.

#### Draconic

Draconic and Lizardfolk architecture might tend towards what we would call mesoAmerican (Olmec, Mayan, and Aztec), with step pyramids and cyclopean walls built from massive, precision-cut but unmortared stones.

The arrogance and narcissism of primordial dragons is usually reflected in their architecture; the dragon’s lair will be the obvious centerpiece of any structures built by their worshipers.

#### Qlippothic

Qlipphotic architecture is strange. “NonEuclidean” is a word that often gets thrown about; qlippothic structures will seemingly bend the laws of physics, producing impossible angles, structures whose insides are larger than their outsides, and curves that somehow seem to converge and diverge simultaneously. Often, qlippothic architecture will have strange organic shapes that seem to combine living and non-living features.

#### Fey

Fey “architecture” is quite often an illusion, copied from the dreams and nightmares of mortal minds. As such, it will typically look like a more fanciful version of normal structures, with details emphasized based on the significance that the structure is intended to convey – a home will seem more homely, a noble’s castle more noble, a dungeon more dark and despairing. Subtle details will be “off” or even nonexistent; Faerie tends to paint in broad strokes.

Even so, a fairy castle or kindly old grandmother’s hovel can be quite convincing, especially to weary travellers looking for a rest and a meal. Of course, the less obviously fey the structure is, the more likely it is to be a trap for the unwary.

#### Orcish

Orcish structures tend to be either brutally functional, or deliberately horrifying. Orcish culture worships power, fear, and violence, and so its architecture is usually designed – when it is designed at all – to inspire these feelings.

Orcish structures that break this mold should give an adventurer pause – whenever orcs have enough time on their hands to devote to something other than pillaging and slaughter, more sinister plots are usually afoot.

#### Barbarian

The word “barbarian”is always a loaded term. Other cultures, of course, have their own definitions for “barbarian”, for which the player characters’ culture themselves might even qualify. For campaigns with a medieval western European aesthetic, barbarian architecture typically means pagan Norse gathering halls and monolithic stone temples.

## Hazards and Traps

Monsters shouldn't be the only threat that a referee throws at an adventuring party, or even the primary threat. Traps and hazards are any aspect of the environment itself that poses a direct threat to the adventuring party, which requires the party's skill and ingenuity to navigate, but is not under the direct control of an active agency such as a monster or NPC.

### Natural Hazards

Natural hazards in the wilderness could be almost anything, from a dangerous whitewater current in a river to a precariously narrow path along a cliff face. Whenever a natural hazard is encountered, think about the description of the terrain provided so far, and then determine the nature of the trap so that it fits into that terrain.

Natural hazards in a dungeon depend greatly on the dungeon itself. Most dungeon ruins are vast technological wonders slowly falling into decay; causeways and scaffolds and pillars designed to be maintained or replaced every few decades have been rusting unattended for over a millennium.

A natural hazard will typically require an Agility check to successfully navigate; clever players might improvise something with ropes, grappling hooks, or the like to lower the difficulty. Deciding to go around might be a Survival or Alertness check to find an alternate route, if one even exists.

Each player will typically need to overcome the hazard individually, unless the party comes up with a clever solution to neutralize the hazard altogether. If any member of the party fails, the entire party is stuck in this hex until they all succeed; if a member of the party rolls a critical failure, think of a suitable mishap and present it to the party to deal with.

### Clever Traps

Kobolds and goblins are notorious for taking over a ruin, filling it with traps, and then waiting to ambush the unwary. Many dungeons were even trapped by their original builders, before they fell into ruin; such devices are notorious for their cleverness and lethality.

Hunters, barbarian warbands, and even some clever beasts and monsters might set up deliberate traps in a particular area. If the party stumbles onto a trap, you should consider the Intelligence modifier and crafting proficiencies of the creature that set it; this determines the difficulty of any task checks to detect, inspect, and defeat the trap.

Not all traps are hidden, but if a trap is hidden, then the first step for the party is detecting it. Failure to detect the trap means that it is likely to go off as the group moves through its area.

Detecting a hidden trap means that someone needs to be specifically searching for them. Let each person who is searching for traps make an active Alertness test, with a difficulty based on the trap’s detection rating. This d6 roll should be made in secret, so as to not give away to the party that something is afoot.

Once the trap is discovered, disabling it requires a Science test to figure out how it works, followed by a Finesse test to disable it. A key thing to remember with both traps and hazards is that the logic of the situation trumps any particular rules or rolls. If the party describes something that will reliably disable a trap, let them disable the trap without rolling. If they describe an action that is likely to work, make the roll easy; if they want to try something that is unlikely to work, make the roll hard.

Also remember that the entire purpose of simple task checks is to make the actual dicerolling part of the game fast and fun, and to avoid bogging down the whole session with needless accounting.

## Describing Magic

Magic is usually a core theme in most fantasy games, and managing it properly as a referee requires quite a bit of forethought, imagination, and logic all blended together. Primal and Divine magic in the default setting comes principally from the yin and yang worlds - Faerie and Shadow – and each of these sources flavors the magic accordingly. Primal magic, which comes entirely from Faerie, grounds itself out through a prepared piece of elemental or fey materia - a solidified ‘gem’ of pure magical energy that resonates with one of the Elemental Chaos’s five pure elemental forms, or (for fey materia) with the pure thoughts, ideas, and fantasies of the Faerie realm itself. On the other side is divine magic, which comes entirely from Shadow; the curses, blessings, and divinations of a miracle-worker typically have no elemental affinities at all, but directly channel the hopes, fears, and memories of the collective unconscious.

Arcane magic, on the other hand, uses the interface between the yin and yang worlds and our own to shape the energies stored directly within the materia itself – thus affording, and requiring, a level of precision and control that Divine and Primal magics lack. Because an arcane spell or magic item must shape and channel these energies directly, the “circuitry” of the spell must be explicitly laid out in the material world - either by painting that circuitry directly on the spellcaster’s body (thus creating a Wizard spell or a Dwarven dweomer), or by engraving it into a material object that also serves as a physical focus (thus creating a magic item).

A spell is a sufficiently raw release of energy that its activation completely burns out the materia used to power it – the ink patterns painted on the wizard’s body literally burn off in a harmless display of ghostly fire – but most magic items are built to sufficient tolerances that their materia is merely depleted, rather than destroyed, when their effect is activated. Items powered by dragon pearls exploit the pearl’s resonant connection with the Elemental Chaos to draw energy back into it, recharging the item over a period of time from a few minutes to a full day.

This resonant connection waxes and wanes over the course of a day – it is strongest at precisely the moment the sun crosses the horizon, at sunrise and sunset – which is why the two-hour window around these moments is ideal for preparing spells and miracles.

Another peak happens around noon and at midnight, but these secondary peaks are harder to predict and control.

Elemental magic, whether arcane or primal, manifests into one of five classical ‘elements’. These elements are often arranged into a “cross of opposition”, with Flame opposing Water and Air opposing Stone, and Wood (or "Life" or "Vitality") unopposed in the center. Other schools of arcane magic attempt to create a “circle of opposition”, with Flame opposing Wood and Water, Air opposing Water and Stone, Wood opposing Flame and Stone, and Water opposing Flame and Air.

Both arrangements have merit; if players attempt to use elemental oppositions in a particularly clever way, try to think through the actual physical nature of the effects and then do what seems logically consistent and scientifically sound.

Non-elemental arcane magic is either an aetheric or illusion effect, with primal magic combining both these schools into the fey category. Aetheric spells directly manipulate the fabric of time and space, allowing for teleportation, co-location, divination, and other strange effects. Illusion magics directly manipulate the thoughts, emotions, and perceptions of thinking and feeling beings.

Fey magics do both, but typically do so through a very different mechanism than their arcane equivalents – an arcane beguilement operates by directly modifying how a mind receives and processes information, while a fey beguilement simply floods that mind with half-shaped energies from Faerie and then lets those energies do the rest of the work. As a result, fey spells typically have a much more ‘storybook’-like logic to them, with the referee much freer to innovate or pull the spell’s effects in thematically appropriate directions, while aetheric and illusion spells typically give the wizards who cast them exactly what they ask for, no more or less.

<!-- p. 232 -->
When describing a magical spell, a good referee will try to provide a diverse range of visual, auditory, and other sensory cues.

Wizard spells in particular tend to manifest in extremely cinematic ways – a divination spell might begin as a drawing of a closed eye on the wizard’s forehead, painted in black and purple magic ink, which ‘opens up’ into an animated third eye that actively turns its gaze to look where directed. An elemental attack spell might begin as a pair of half-circles painted onto the palms of each of the wizard’s hands, encircled with runes, with one rune missing - which is painted on the wizard’s opposite thumbnail. When the wizard places both hands together, palms outwards, the two halves of the circle align into a full conjuration sigil - and when the wizard folds their thumb over to align it with the missing space, the entire sigil alights in ghostly fire (colored per the conjuration’s element) expanding into a large glowing circle suspended and rotating in front of the wizard’s hands, which contracts and fires bolts of elemental energies whenever the mage shouts a command word. The cantrip effect might be activated by holding the hands together and speaking the command word, but without folding over the thumb to complete the runes.

Primal glamours can be just as cinematic, but most of the impressive special effects tend to happen at the location of the spell itself. A druid’s elemental spells will often show echoes of the fey spirits that are helping to shape them – a crashing wave of water might have watery horses manifesting at the wavepeaks, or a blazing inferno might shape itself into a raging, fiery bull for brief moments. Divine miracles, by comparison, are much more subtle. Other than the glowing prayer strip and the priest’s loudly chanted or quietly murmured prayer, there may be little evidence at all that a first-circle magical effect is even being invoked. Second- and thirdcircle effects tend to involve some amount of energetic display, but even these miracles typically limit themselves to auras of light, halos shining around the recipients of blessings or flashes of fiery wrath encircling the victims of curses.

### Using Magic Creatively

Spells, miracles, and glamours tend to have specifically-defined effects. However, a creative spellcaster should be able to use them for more than their intended purpose. For example, an elemental attack spell that deals fire damage could certainly be used to light flammable substances, or a thunder or aetheric spell that can push or knock creatures over might be used to attempt to move a large object.

Like any creative action, it is your job as the referee to determine whether such an action is impossible, automatic, or requires some kind of task check or saving throw at hard, normal, or easy difficulty.  If the task requires finegrained manipulation of magical energies, it can probably be determined with a Science check. If it requires extensive understanding of natural phenomena, a Survival check might do instead. Tricking intelligent creatures to perform specific actions by generating particularly well-crafted illusions might involve a Wit check, while tricking wild animals in the same manner might necessitate a Survival check. Again, don’t be afraid to decide that a particular attempt is impossible or automatic; actually clever plans should always be rewarded at a higher rate than good dice rolls, and even the best dice rolls shouldn’t save the party from a terrible idea.

### Side Effects of Magic

These same creative applications of magic should be allowed to happen accidentally sometimes, as well. Damaging flame spells should absolutely light fragile flammable objects on fire; acid should absolutely mar or even destroy artwork, scrolls or other delicate objects; aetheric manipulations might have strange effects on other ongoing or ambient aetheric effects, especially those involving portals or other interdimensional shenanigans. (Notice the dangers of placing an aetheric blade within a bag of holding to get an idea of what this might mean.)

As the referee, your job is not to regularly punish the players for failing to understand obscure occult lore; instead, your job is to simulate a dangerous world that is also full of potential, and let the players learn how to engage with it creatively.
